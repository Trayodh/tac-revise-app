### I. Understanding Surds: Types and Definitions

Surds are irrational numbers that can be expressed in the form $\sqrt[n]{x}$, where $x$ is a rational number and $n$ is a positive integer, such that $\sqrt[n]{x}$ is irrational.

#### A. Types of Surds

1.  **Pure Surds:**
    *   **Definition:** A surd which has only 1 as a rational factor.
    *   **Examples:** $\sqrt{2}$, $\sqrt[3]{3}$, $\sqrt[4]{4}$

2.  **Mixed Surds:**
    *   **Definition:** A surd which is not a pure surd or has a rational factor other than unity.
    *   **Examples:** $2\sqrt{3}$, $5\sqrt[3]{12}$, $4\sqrt[3]{35}$

3.  **Like Surds:**
    *   **Definition:** When the radicands (the number under the radical sign) of two surds are the same.
    *   **Examples:** $6\sqrt{3}$ and $2\sqrt{3}$

4.  **Unlike Surds:**
    *   **Definition:** When the radicands of two surds are different.
    *   **Examples:** $4\sqrt{7}$ and $6\sqrt{5}$

### II. Comparison of Two Surds

Comparing surds involves determining which one is larger.

1.  **Surds of the Same Order:**
    *   **Rule:** If two surds are of the same order (the 'n' in $\sqrt[n]{x}$), then the one whose radicand is larger, is the larger of the two.
    *   **Examples:** $\sqrt{17} > \sqrt{13}$, $\sqrt[3]{21} > \sqrt[3]{16}$

2.  **Surds of Different Orders:**
    *   **Rule:** If any two surds have different orders are to be compared, then we will first reduce them to the same but smallest order and then compare them.
    *   **Note:** Let $\sqrt[n]{x}$ be a surd of order n. Then, $\sqrt[n]{x} = x^{1/n} = x^{m/(mn)} = \sqrt[mn]{x^m}$.
    *   **Example 9:** Convert $\sqrt{2}$ into a surd of order 4.
        *   **Sol. c.** $\sqrt{2} = 2^{1/2} = 2^{(1 \times 2)/(2 \times 2)} = 2^{2/4} = \sqrt[4]{2^2} = \sqrt[4]{4}$
    *   **Example 10:** Consider the following in respect of the numbers $\sqrt{2}$, $\sqrt[3]{3}$, and $\sqrt[6]{6}$.
        *   I. $\sqrt[6]{6}$ is the greatest number.
        *   II. $\sqrt{2}$ is the smallest number.
        *   Which of these statements is/are correct?
        *   **Sol. d.** Taking LCM of 2, 3 and 6 = 12.
            *   Now, $\sqrt{2} = 2^{1/2} = 2^{6/12} = \sqrt[12]{2^6} = \sqrt[12]{64}$
            *   $\sqrt[3]{3} = 3^{1/3} = 3^{4/12} = \sqrt[12]{3^4} = \sqrt[12]{81}$
            *   $\sqrt[6]{6} = 6^{1/6} = 6^{2/12} = \sqrt[12]{6^2} = \sqrt[12]{36}$
            *   Comparing radicands: $81 > 64 > 36$.
            *   So, $\sqrt[3]{3}$ is the greatest number and $\sqrt[6]{6}$ is the smallest number.
            *   Therefore, neither I nor II is correct.

### III. Operations on Surds

#### A. Addition and Subtraction of Surds

*   **Rule:** Addition or subtraction takes place only between like surds, using laws of numbers.
*   **Examples:**
    *   (i) $2\sqrt{3} + 4\sqrt{3} = (2+4)\sqrt{3} = 6\sqrt{3}$
    *   (ii) $6\sqrt{5} - 3\sqrt{2} + 7\sqrt{5} + 4\sqrt{2} = (6\sqrt{5} + 7\sqrt{5}) + (-3\sqrt{2} + 4\sqrt{2}) = 13\sqrt{5} + \sqrt{2}$

#### B. Multiplication and Division of Surds

*   **Rule:** Suppose given surds are $\sqrt[p]{a}$, $\sqrt[q]{b}$ and $\sqrt[r]{c}$, then first find the LCM of p, q and r to make the radial powers of the surds same and then their multiplication and division can be done using following rules.
*   **Laws:**
    *   (i) $\sqrt[n]{x} \times \sqrt[n]{y} = \sqrt[n]{xy}$
    *   (ii) $\frac{\sqrt[n]{x}}{\sqrt[n]{y}} = \sqrt[n]{\frac{x}{y}}$
*   **Example 11:** Simplify $\sqrt{12} \div \sqrt[3]{2} \cdot \sqrt[6]{3}$.
    *   **Sol. d.** Here, the order of $\sqrt{3}$, $\sqrt[3]{2}$ is 2 and 3, respectively. So, LCM 2, 3 and 6 = 6.
        *   $\sqrt{3} = 3^{1/2} = 3^{3/6} = \sqrt[6]{3^3} = \sqrt[6]{27}$
        *   $\sqrt[3]{2} = 2^{1/3} = 2^{2/6} = \sqrt[6]{2^2} = \sqrt[6]{4}$
        *   So, $\sqrt{12} \div \sqrt[3]{2} \cdot \sqrt[6]{3} = \sqrt[6]{12^3} \div \sqrt[6]{2^2} \cdot \sqrt[6]{3}$
        *   $= \sqrt[6]{1728} \div \sqrt[6]{4} \cdot \sqrt[6]{3}$
        *   $= \sqrt[6]{\frac{1728}{4} \times 3} = \sqrt[6]{432 \times 3} = \sqrt[6]{1296}$
        *   $= \left(\frac{12}{27 \times 4}\right)^{1/6} = \left(\frac{12}{108}\right)^{1/6} = \left(\frac{1}{9}\right)^{1/6}$
        *   $= \left(\frac{1}{3^2}\right)^{1/6} = \left(\frac{1}{3}\right)^{2/6} = \left(\frac{1}{3}\right)^{1/3} = \frac{1}{\sqrt[3]{3}}$

### IV. Rationalisation of Surds

*   **Definition:** Process of converting a surd into a rational number is called as rationalisation of surds.
*   **Rationalising Factor (RF):** When the product of two surds is a rational number, then each surd is called a rationalising factor of each other.
*   **Examples:**
    *   $\sqrt[3]{25} \times \sqrt[3]{5} = \sqrt[3]{125} = 5$. So, $\sqrt[3]{25}$ and $\sqrt[3]{5}$ are rationalising factor of each other.
*   **Rationalising factors of a given surd:**
    *   Rationalising factor of $\sqrt{a}$ is $\sqrt{a}$.
    *   Rationalising factor of $\frac{1}{\sqrt{a} \pm \sqrt{b}}$ is $\sqrt{a} \mp \sqrt{b}$.
*   **Example 12:** $\frac{\sqrt{5}+\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{5}-\sqrt{3}} + \frac{\sqrt{5}-\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{5}+\sqrt{3}}$ is equal to
    *   **Sol. b.**
        *   $\frac{\sqrt{5}+\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{5}-\sqrt{3}} + \frac{\sqrt{5}-\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{5}+\sqrt{3}}$
        *   $= \frac{(\sqrt{5}+\sqrt{3})^2 + (\sqrt{5}-\sqrt{3})^2}{(\sqrt{5}-\sqrt{3})(\sqrt{5}+\sqrt{3})}$
        *   Using $(A+B)^2 + (A-B)^2 = 2(A^2+B^2)$ and $(A-B)(A+B) = A^2-B^2$:
        *   $= \frac{2((\sqrt{5})^2 + (\sqrt{3})^2)}{(\sqrt{5})^2 - (\sqrt{3})^2}$
        *   $= \frac{2(5+3)}{5-3} = \frac{2(8)}{2} = 8$.

### V. Deeper Dive into Surds and Roots

#### A. Understanding the 'Order' and 'Radicand'

*   **Order (Index):** The 'n' in $\sqrt[n]{x}$. It specifies the type of root (e.g., square root for n=2, cube root for n=3). A higher order means the number under the radical sign needs to be multiplied by itself 'n' times to yield the original number.
*   **Radicand:** The 'x' in $\sqrt[n]{x}$. This is the number whose root is being taken.
*   **Implication for Pure/Mixed Surds:** For a surd to be 'pure', its radicand must not contain any perfect $n^{th}$ power factors other than 1. For instance, $\sqrt{18}$ is a mixed surd because $18 = 9 \times 2 = 3^2 \times 2$, which simplifies to $3\sqrt{2}$. Here, $3\sqrt{2}$ is a mixed surd, and $\sqrt{2}$ is a pure surd.

#### B. Practical Tips for Comparing Surds

*   **The Power of LCM:** When comparing surds of different orders, the most reliable method is to convert them all to a common order, preferably the Least Common Multiple (LCM) of their original orders. This transforms the comparison into a straightforward comparison of their radicands under the same root.
    *   **Mnemonic:** "LCM for Like Comparison" – use the LCM of the orders to make the surds 'like' in their structure for easy comparison.
*   **Avoid Decimal Approximations:** For competitive exams, relying on decimal approximations of surds can lead to errors, especially when values are close. The LCM method provides an exact comparison.

#### C. Common Pitfalls in Surd Operations

1.  **Adding/Subtracting Unlike Surds:** A frequent error is attempting to add or subtract the radicands of unlike surds (e.g., $\sqrt{5} + \sqrt{2} \neq \sqrt{7}$). Remember, only surds with identical radicands and orders (like surds) can be combined.
2.  **Incorrect Order Conversion:** When converting $\sqrt[n]{x}$ to $\sqrt[kn]{x^k}$, ensure that both the order 'n' and the power of the radicand 'x' are multiplied by the same factor 'k'.
3.  **Distributing Roots Incorrectly:** A fundamental algebraic error is assuming $\sqrt{a+b} = \sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b}$. This is incorrect.
4.  **Simplifying Before Operations:** Always simplify mixed surds to their simplest pure surd form (e.g., $\sqrt{27} = 3\sqrt{3}$) before performing addition or subtraction. This helps in identifying like surds that can be combined.

#### D. Rationalisation: Why and How

*   **Purpose of Rationalisation:** The primary reason for rationalising the denominator of a fraction containing surds is to simplify the expression and adhere to mathematical convention, which generally prefers rational denominators. It also makes further calculations (like addition or subtraction of such fractions) easier.
*   **Conjugate Pairs:** For binomial surds in the denominator, such as $\sqrt{a} \pm \sqrt{b}$, the rationalising factor is its conjugate pair, $\sqrt{a} \mp \sqrt{b}$. This technique leverages the difference of squares identity, $(X-Y)(X+Y) = X^2-Y^2$, to eliminate the surds from the denominator.
    *   **Example:** To rationalise $\frac{1}{3-\sqrt{2}}$:
        *   Multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate: $\frac{1}{3-\sqrt{2}} \times \frac{3+\sqrt{2}}{3+\sqrt{2}}$
        *   $= \frac{3+\sqrt{2}}{3^2 - (\sqrt{2})^2} = \frac{3+\sqrt{2}}{9-2} = \frac{3+\sqrt{2}}{7}$.

### VI. Square Roots and Cube Roots (General Concepts)

The textbook introduces questions involving square roots and cube roots. Here are foundational concepts for these.

*   **Square Root ($\sqrt{x}$ or $\sqrt[2]{x}$):** A number 'a' is the square root of 'x' if $a^2 = x$. Every positive number has two square roots (a positive principal root and a negative root). $\sqrt{x}$ conventionally refers to the principal (positive) square root.
    *   **Perfect Squares:** Integers whose square roots are also integers (e.g., 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, ...).
    *   **Unit Digits of Perfect Squares:** A perfect square can only end in 0, 1, 4, 5, 6, or 9. It can never end in 2, 3, 7, or 8. This property is useful for quickly eliminating options in MCQs.
*   **Cube Root ($\sqrt[3]{x}$):** A number 'a' is the cube root of 'x' if $a^3 = x$. Every real number has exactly one real cube root.
    *   **Perfect Cubes:** Integers whose cube roots are also integers (e.g., 1, 8, 27, 64, 125, ...).
    *   **Unit Digits of Perfect Cubes:** The unit digit of a perfect cube can be any digit from 0-9. There's a unique mapping for unit digits: 0->0, 1->1, 2->8, 3->7, 4->4, 5->5, 6->6, 7->3, 8->2, 9->9.
