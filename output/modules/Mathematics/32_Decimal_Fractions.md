### I. Introduction to Decimal Fractions

A decimal fraction is a fraction whose denominator is a power of ten (e.g., 10, 100, 1000). The decimal point separates the whole number part from the fractional part.

### II. Types of Decimal Fractions

1.  **Terminating Decimal:** A decimal that ends after a finite number of digits.
    *   **Example:** $13/8 = 1.625$, $3/16 = 0.1875$, $137/25 = 5.48$
    *   **Characteristic:** Denominator (in simplest form) has only prime factors of 2 and/or 5.

2.  **Non-Terminating Decimal:** A decimal that continues infinitely.
    *   **Sub-types:**
        *   **Pure Recurring Decimal:** All digits after the decimal point repeat.
            *   **Example:** $0.777... = 0.\overline{7}$, $0.786786... = 0.\overline{786}$
            *   **Characteristic:** The repeating block starts immediately after the decimal point.
        *   **Mixed Recurring Decimal:** Some digits after the decimal point are not repeated, while some are repeated.
            *   **Example:** $0.17999... = 0.17\overline{9}$, $0.053939... = 0.05\overline{39}$
            *   **Characteristic:** A non-repeating block exists between the decimal point and the repeating block.

**Note:** The decimal expansion of a rational number is either terminating or non-terminating recurring. Conversely, any number whose decimal expansion is terminating or non-terminating recurring is a rational number.

### III. Operations on Decimal Fractions

#### A. Addition and Subtraction
*   **Rule:** Align the decimal points vertically. Add or subtract digits as usual, carrying over or borrowing as needed.
*   **Key Point:** Annexing zeroes to the extreme right of a decimal fraction does not change its value (e.g., $3.57 = 3.570$).

    *   **Example (Subtraction):**
        ```
        7.093
      - 3.570
      -------
        3.523
        ```
    *   **Example (Addition):**
        ```
        51.300
         7.078
         1.380
       + 0.900
       -------
        60.658
        ```

#### B. Multiplication
*   **Rule:**
    1.  Ignore the decimal points and multiply the numbers as if they were whole numbers.
    2.  Count the total number of decimal places in all the numbers being multiplied.
    3.  In the product, place the decimal point from the right, counting as many places as the total calculated in step 2.

    *   **Example:** $5 \times 0.25 \times 6.301 \times 0.00394$
        1.  Multiply $5 \times 25 \times 6301 \times 394 = 310324250$
        2.  Total decimal places: $0 (from 5) + 2 (from 0.25) + 3 (from 6.301) + 5 (from 0.00394) = 10$
        3.  Result: $0.0310324250$

#### C. Division
*   **Rule 1 (Decimal by Natural Number):**
    1.  Divide the number without the decimal point by the natural number.
    2.  In the quotient, place the decimal point after as many places from the right as there were in the dividend.

    *   **Example:** $0.01834 \div 13$
        1.  $1834 \div 13 = 141.077$ (considering it as a whole number division initially)
        2.  Dividend $0.01834$ has 7 decimal places.
        3.  Result: $0.00141077$

*   **Rule 2 (Decimal by Decimal):**
    1.  Multiply both the dividend and the divisor by a suitable power of 10 (e.g., 10, 100, 1000) to make the divisor a whole number.
    2.  Then, proceed as per Rule 1.

    *   **Example:** $0.0066 \div 0.22$
        1.  Multiply both by $100$: $(0.0066 \times 100) \div (0.22 \times 100) = 0.66 \div 22$
        2.  Now, divide $0.66$ by $22$:
            *   $66 \div 22 = 3$
            *   Dividend $0.66$ has 2 decimal places.
        3.  Result: $0.03$

### IV. Conversion of Recurring Decimals to Simple Fractions

#### A. Pure Recurring Decimal
*   **Rule:**
    1.  Write the repeating figures only once in the numerator (without the decimal point).
    2.  Write as many nines in the denominator as the number of repeating figures.

    *   **Example:**
        *   $0.\overline{5} = 5/9$
        *   $0.\overline{45} = 45/99$
        *   $1.\overline{27} = 1 + 0.\overline{27} = 1 + 27/99 = 1 + 3/11 = 14/11$

#### B. Mixed Recurring Decimal
*   **Rule:**
    1.  **Numerator:** Form a number using all digits after the decimal point (taking repeated digits only once). Subtract the number formed by the non-repeating digits from this.
    2.  **Denominator:** Place as many nines as there are repeating digits, followed by as many zeroes as there are non-repeating digits.

    *   **Example:** $0.12\overline{36}$
        1.  All digits after decimal (repeated once): $1236$. Non-repeating digits: $12$. Numerator: $1236 - 12 = 1224$.
        2.  Repeating digits: $2$ (36). Non-repeating digits: $2$ (12). Denominator: $9900$.
        3.  Result: $1224/9900 = 102/825$

### V. Comparison of Fractions

#### A. By Converting to Decimal Form
*   **Rule:** Convert each fraction into its decimal equivalent. Then, arrange them in ascending or descending order based on their decimal values.

    *   **Example:** Arrange $2/3, 5/6, 16/25, 3/7$ in ascending order.
        *   $2/3 \approx 0.666$
        *   $5/6 \approx 0.833$
        *   $16/25 = 0.64$
        *   $3/7 \approx 0.428571$
        *   Ascending order: $0.428571 < 0.64 < 0.666 < 0.833$
        *   So, $3/7 < 16/25 < 2/3 < 5/6$

#### B. By Equating Denominators
*   **Rule:**
    1.  Find the LCM of all the denominators.
    2.  Convert each fraction to an equivalent fraction with the LCM as the new denominator.
    3.  The fraction with the largest numerator will be the largest fraction (and vice-versa).

    *   **Example:** Arrange $7/8, 5/6, 6/7$ in descending order.
        1.  LCM of $8, 6, 7 = 168$.
        2.  Convert fractions:
            *   $7/8 = (7 \times 21)/(8 \times 21) = 147/168$
            *   $5/6 = (5 \times 28)/(6 \times 28) = 140/168$
            *   $6/7 = (6 \times 24)/(7 \times 24) = 144/168$
        3.  Compare numerators: $147 > 144 > 140$.
        4.  Descending order: $147/168 > 144/168 > 140/168$
        5.  So, $7/8 > 6/7 > 5/6$

### VI. HCF and LCM of Decimal Fractions

*   **Rule:**
    1.  Make all given decimal fractions have the same number of decimal places by annexing zeroes if necessary.
    2.  Ignore the decimal points and find the HCF or LCM of the resulting whole numbers.
    3.  In the final result, place the decimal point from the right, counting as many places as were made common in step 1.

    *   **Example:** Find HCF and LCM of $1.600, 8, 2.4$.
        1.  Equate decimal places: $1.600, 8.000, 2.400$. (3 decimal places)
        2.  Consider as whole numbers: $1600, 8000, 2400$.
        3.  HCF of $(1600, 8000, 2400) = 800$.
        4.  LCM of $(1600, 8000, 2400) = 24000$.
        5.  Place decimal point (3 places from right):
            *   HCF: $0.800$
            *   LCM: $24.000$

### I. Rational Numbers and Decimal Expansions: A Deeper Look

The textbook states a crucial property: **"The decimal expansion of a rational number is either terminating or non-terminating recurring."** This is a fundamental concept for competitive exams.

*   **Why does this happen?** When you divide a numerator by a denominator, there are only a finite number of possible remainders (less than the denominator).
    *   If a remainder of 0 is reached, the decimal terminates.
    *   If a remainder repeats, the sequence of digits in the quotient will also repeat, leading to a non-terminating recurring decimal.

*   **Identifying Terminating Decimals (Quick Check):**
    A fraction $p/q$ (in its simplest form) will result in a terminating decimal if and only if the prime factors of its denominator $q$ are only 2s and/or 5s.
    *   **Example:** $3/16 = 3/(2^4)$ (terminating). $137/25 = 137/(5^2)$ (terminating).
    *   **Example:** $3/11$ (denominator 11, not 2 or 5) will be non-terminating recurring. $2/3$ (denominator 3, not 2 or 5) will be non-terminating recurring.

### II. Mnemonics and Formulas for Recurring Decimal Conversions

Converting recurring decimals to fractions is a common exam question. Here are simplified mnemonics based on the textbook rules:

#### A. Pure Recurring Decimal: "Repeat over Nines"
*   **Mnemonic:** "Digits Repeat? Put 'em over Nines, complete!"
*   **Formula:** $0.\overline{D_1 D_2 ... D_k} = \frac{D_1 D_2 ... D_k}{\underbrace{99...9}_{k \text{ times}}}$
    *   Where $D_1 D_2 ... D_k$ is the number formed by the repeating digits, and $k$ is the number of repeating digits.
*   **Example:**
    *   $0.\overline{7} = 7/9$ (1 repeating digit, 1 nine)
    *   $0.\overline{45} = 45/99$ (2 repeating digits, 2 nines)
    *   $0.\overline{786} = 786/999$ (3 repeating digits, 3 nines)

#### B. Mixed Recurring Decimal: "All Minus Non-Repeat, Over Nines and Zeroes"
*   **Mnemonic:** "All digits minus non-repeat, over nines for repeat, zeroes for non-repeat, that's the feat!"
*   **Formula:** $0.N_1 N_2 ... N_m \overline{R_1 R_2 ... R_k} = \frac{(N_1 N_2 ... N_m R_1 R_2 ... R_k) - (N_1 N_2 ... N_m)}{\underbrace{99...9}_{k \text{ times}} \underbrace{00...0}_{m \text{ times}}}$
    *   Where $N_1 N_2 ... N_m$ is the number formed by the non-repeating digits, $R_1 R_2 ... R_k$ is the number formed by the repeating digits.
    *   The numerator is (Number formed by all digits after decimal, repeated once) - (Number formed by non-repeating digits).
    *   The denominator has $k$ nines (for $k$ repeating digits) followed by $m$ zeroes (for $m$ non-repeating digits).
*   **Example:**
    *   $0.17\overline{9}$
        *   All digits (repeated once): $179$. Non-repeating: $17$. Numerator: $179 - 17 = 162$.
        *   Repeating digits: $1$ (9). Non-repeating digits: $2$ (17). Denominator: $900$.
        *   Result: $162/900 = 9/50$
    *   $0.05\overline{39}$
        *   All digits (repeated once): $0539 \rightarrow 539$. Non-repeating: $05 \rightarrow 5$. Numerator: $539 - 5 = 534$.
        *   Repeating digits: $2$ (39). Non-repeating digits: $2$ (05). Denominator: $9900$.
        *   Result: $534/9900 = 89/1650$

### III. Deep Dive into Decimal Operations

#### A. Addition and Subtraction: The Importance of Alignment
The rule "decimal points lie in one column" is critical. It ensures you are adding/subtracting corresponding place values (tenths with tenths, hundredths with hundredths, etc.). Annexing zeroes helps visualize this alignment, especially when numbers have different numbers of decimal places.

#### B. Multiplication: Counting Decimal Places
The logic behind counting decimal places in multiplication comes from powers of 10.
*   $0.5 \times 0.03 = (5/10) \times (3/100) = 15/1000 = 0.015$
*   Notice: $0.5$ (1 decimal place) and $0.03$ (2 decimal places) give a product with $1+2=3$ decimal places. This rule generalizes for any number of factors.

#### C. Division: Making the Divisor a Whole Number
This technique simplifies division by effectively shifting the decimal point in both numbers.
*   $0.0066 \div 0.22$
*   This is equivalent to $\frac{0.0066}{0.22}$.
*   To make the denominator a whole number, multiply numerator and denominator by $100$: $\frac{0.0066 \times 100}{0.22 \times 100} = \frac{0.66}{22}$.
*   This doesn't change the value of the fraction, but it makes the division easier to perform.

### IV. HCF and LCM of Decimals: The "Whole Number" Trick

The method of converting decimals to whole numbers by equating decimal places and then finding HCF/LCM is a powerful simplification.
*   **Example:** HCF of $1.6, 8, 2.4$
    *   Convert to same decimal places: $1.600, 8.000, 2.400$.
    *   Consider as whole numbers: $1600, 8000, 2400$.
    *   HCF of $(1600, 8000, 2400) = 800$.
    *   Since we effectively multiplied each number by $1000$ (to shift decimal 3 places), we must divide the final HCF by $1000$. So, $800/1000 = 0.800$.
*   This method works because HCF(a, b, c) = HCF(ka, kb, kc) / k, and LCM(a, b, c) = LCM(ka, kb, kc) / k, where k is a common factor (in this case, a power of 10).
