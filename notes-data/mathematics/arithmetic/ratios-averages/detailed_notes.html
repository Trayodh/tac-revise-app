Part 1: Ratios – The Language of Comparison



1.1 Introduction to Ratios

A ratio is a comparison of two or more quantities of the same kind, expressed in the simplest form. It indicates how many times one quantity contains another or is contained in another. Ratios are dimensionless, meaning they do not have units, as the units of the quantities being compared cancel out.

* Notation: A ratio between two quantities, 'a' and 'b', is typically written as:
* a : b (read as "a is to b")
* a/b (fraction form)
* a to b
* Antecedent and Consequent: In the ratio a:b, 'a' is called the antecedent and 'b' is called the consequent.
* Key Principle: A ratio represents a relationship between parts of a whole or between different quantities. It does not tell us the actual values of the quantities, only their relative sizes.

Example: If there are 10 boys and 15 girls in a class, the ratio of boys to girls is 10:15, which simplifies to 2:3. This means for every 2 boys, there are 3 girls.

1.2 Properties of Ratios

1. Simplification: Ratios are always expressed in their simplest form by dividing both the antecedent and consequent by their Highest Common Factor (HCF).
* Example: 20:30 simplifies to 2:3.
2. Multiplication/Division by a Constant: Multiplying or dividing both terms of a ratio by the same non-zero number does not change the ratio's value.
* If a:b = 2:3, then 2k:3k also represents the same ratio for any k ≠ 0. This property is crucial for solving problems where actual values are derived from ratios.
3. Order Matters: The ratio a:b is generally not the same as b:a, unless a=b.
4. Units: Quantities being compared must be of the same kind and in the same units. If not, they must be converted to the same units before forming a ratio.
* Example: Ratio of 50 paise to 2 rupees = 50 paise : 200 paise = 1:4.

1.3 Types of Ratios

1. Compound Ratio: The compound ratio of two or more ratios is obtained by multiplying their antecedents and consequents separately.
* Compound ratio of (a:b) and (c:d) is (ac : bd).
* Example: Compound ratio of 2:3 and 4:5 is (2x4) : (3x5) = 8:15.
2. Duplicate Ratio: The duplicate ratio of a:b is a²:b².
* Example: Duplicate ratio of 2:3 is 2²:3² = 4:9.
3. Sub-duplicate Ratio: The sub-duplicate ratio of a:b is √a:√b.
* Example: Sub-duplicate ratio of 4:9 is √4:√9 = 2:3.
4. Triplicate Ratio: The triplicate ratio of a:b is a³:b³.
* Example: Triplicate ratio of 2:3 is 2³:3³ = 8:27.
5. Sub-triplicate Ratio: The sub-triplicate ratio of a:b is ³√a:³√b.
* Example: Sub-triplicate ratio of 8:27 is ³√8:³√27 = 2:3.
6. Inverse Ratio (Reciprocal Ratio): The inverse ratio of a:b is b:a or 1/a : 1/b.
* Example: Inverse ratio of 2:3 is 3:2.

1.4 Proportion

A proportion is an equality of two ratios. If a:b = c:d, then a, b, c, and d are said to be in proportion. This is often written as a:b :: c:d.

* Extremes and Means: In a:b :: c:d, 'a' and 'd' are called the extremes, and 'b' and 'c' are called the means.
* Product Rule: In a proportion, the product of the extremes is equal to the product of the means.
* If a:b :: c:d, then ad = bc. This is a fundamental property used extensively in problem-solving.

1.4.1 Types of Proportion

1. Direct Proportion: Two quantities are in direct proportion if an increase in one quantity leads to a proportional increase in the other, and vice-versa.
* Example: Cost of items and number of items. If 10 pens cost ₹50, then 20 pens will cost ₹100.
2. Inverse Proportion: Two quantities are in inverse proportion if an increase in one quantity leads to a proportional decrease in the other, and vice-versa.
* Example: Speed and time taken to cover a fixed distance. If speed increases, time decreases.

1.4.2 Continued Proportion

Three quantities a, b, c are in continued proportion if a:b = b:c. In this case, b is called the mean proportional between a and c.
* From a:b = b:c, we get b² = ac, so b = √ac.

1.4.3 Mean, Third, and Fourth Proportional

* Mean Proportional: If a, b, c are in continued proportion (a:b = b:c), then b is the mean proportional between a and c. (b = √ac)
* Third Proportional: If a, b, c are in continued proportion, then c is the third proportional to a and b. (c = b²/a)
* Fourth Proportional: If a:b = c:d, then d is the fourth proportional to a, b, and c. (d = bc/a)

1.5 High-Yield Concepts & Problem-Solving Strategies for Ratios

1. Combining Ratios: To combine ratios like A:B and B:C into A:B:C, make the common term (B) equal in both ratios by finding its LCM.
* Example: A:B = 2:3, B:C = 4:5.
* LCM of 3 and 4 is 12.
* A:B = (2x4):(3x4) = 8:12
* B:C = (4x3):(5x3) = 12:15
* So, A:B:C = 8:12:15.
* [Diagram: A simple visual representation of combining ratios using blocks or segments, where the common part 'B' is adjusted to be of equal size in both comparisons.]
2. Componendo and Dividendo Rule: If a/b = c/d, then (a+b)/(a-b) = (c+d)/(c-d). This rule is extremely useful in simplifying complex algebraic ratio problems.
3. Cross-Multiplication Method: For problems involving changes in ratios (e.g., "If X people are added to group A and Y people to group B, the ratio changes from P:Q to R:S"), the cross-multiplication method can be very efficient.
* Let initial quantities be Px and Qx.
* (Px + X) / (Qx + Y) = R/S
* This leads to S(Px + X) = R(Qx + Y), which can be solved for x.
4. Unitary Method: Often, if a ratio is given (e.g., A:B = 2:3) and the actual value of one part is known (e.g., A = 10), then one 'unit' of the ratio can be found (2 units = 10, so 1 unit = 5). Then B = 3 units = 15.

1.6 Applications of Ratios

* Age Problems: Determining current or future ages based on given ratios.
* Mixture Problems: Calculating the quantities of components in a mixture or the effect of adding/removing components.
* Partnership Problems: Distributing profits or losses among partners based on their investment ratios and time periods.
* Time and Work: Ratios of efficiencies can be used to determine the ratio of time taken or work done.
* Data Interpretation: Ratios are fundamental for analyzing and comparing data presented in tables, graphs, and charts. For example, comparing male-to-female ratios in different sectors.

Part 2: Averages – The Central Tendency



2.1 Introduction to Averages (Arithmetic Mean)

An average, specifically the Arithmetic Mean (AM), is a single value that represents the central tendency of a set of numbers. It is calculated by summing all the values in the set and dividing by the count of those values.

* Formula: Average (Mean) = (Sum of all observations) / (Number of observations)
* Symbolically, if x₁, x₂, ..., xₙ are 'n' observations, then Mean (x̄) = (x₁ + x₂ + ... + xₙ) / n = Σxᵢ / n
* Purpose: Averages help in understanding the typical value of a dataset, making comparisons, and summarizing large amounts of data into a single, representative figure.

Example: The scores of a student in 5 subjects are 70, 80, 60, 90, 75.
Average score = (70+80+60+90+75) / 5 = 375 / 5 = 75.

2.2 Properties of Averages

1. Effect of Addition/Subtraction: If each observation in a dataset is increased or decreased by a constant 'k', the average also increases or decreases by 'k'.
2. Effect of Multiplication/Division: If each observation in a dataset is multiplied or divided by a constant 'k', the average also gets multiplied or divided by 'k'.
3. Zero Sum of Deviations: The sum of deviations of all observations from their arithmetic mean is always zero. Σ(xᵢ - x̄) = 0. This illustrates that the mean is a balancing point.
* [Diagram: A number line with several points (observations) and the mean marked as a fulcrum, showing that the sum of distances to the left equals the sum of distances to the right.]
4. Mean of Combined Groups: If we have two groups with n₁ observations and mean x̄₁, and n₂ observations and mean x̄₂, the combined mean is:
* Combined Mean = (n₁x̄₁ + n₂x̄₂) / (n₁ + n₂)

2.3 Weighted Average

A weighted average is used when different observations in a dataset have different levels of importance or frequency. Each observation is multiplied by its corresponding weight, and the sum of these products is divided by the sum of the weights.

* Formula: Weighted Average = (w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + ... + wₙxₙ) / (w₁ + w₂ + ... + wₙ) = Σ(wᵢxᵢ) / Σwᵢ
* Here, xᵢ are the observations and wᵢ are their respective weights.
* Applications: Calculating average marks where different subjects have different credit points, average cost of items purchased at different prices and quantities, average speed (where time is the weight).

Example: A student scores 80 in a 3-credit subject and 90 in a 2-credit subject.
Weighted Average Score = (80x3 + 90x2) / (3+2) = (240 + 180) / 5 = 420 / 5 = 84.

2.4 Types of Means (Brief Overview for UPSC Context)

While Arithmetic Mean (AM) is the most common, UPSC aspirants should be aware of others:

1. Arithmetic Mean (AM): (a+b)/2 for two numbers. General formula as above.
2. Geometric Mean (GM): For 'n' positive numbers, GM = ⁿ√(x₁ x₂ ... * xₙ). Used for averaging rates of change, growth rates, etc.
3. Harmonic Mean (HM): For 'n' positive numbers, HM = n / (1/x₁ + 1/x₂ + ... + 1/xₙ). Used for averaging rates (like speed, work rates) when the quantity being averaged is expressed as a rate per unit of another quantity (e.g., km/hr).
* Relationship: For a given set of positive numbers, AM ≥ GM ≥ HM. Equality holds only if all numbers are identical.

2.5 High-Yield Concepts & Problem-Solving Strategies for Averages

1. Deviation Method (Assumed Mean Method): When numbers are large, assume an average (A) close to the values. Calculate deviations (dᵢ = xᵢ - A) for each observation.
* Actual Average = Assumed Average + (Sum of deviations / Number of observations)
* This simplifies calculations significantly.
* [Diagram: A table showing observations, an assumed mean, and calculated deviations, leading to the final average.]
2. Concept of Inclusion/Exclusion:
* Adding a new observation: If a new observation 'x' is added to 'n' observations with average 'A', the new average is (nA + x) / (n+1).
* Removing an observation: If an observation 'x' is removed from 'n' observations with average 'A', the new average is (nA - x) / (n-1).
* Replacing an observation: If an observation 'x' is replaced by 'y' in 'n' observations with average 'A', the new average is A + (y-x)/n. The change in average is (y-x)/n. This is a very common type of problem.
3. Average Speed: This is a classic application of weighted average or HM.
* If a person travels distance D₁ at speed S₁ and D₂ at speed S₂, average speed = (D₁ + D₂) / (D₁/S₁ + D₂/S₂).
* If a person travels the same distance 'D' at speed S₁ and then returns the same distance 'D' at speed S₂, the average speed = 2D / (D/S₁ + D/S₂) = 2S₁S₂ / (S₁ + S₂). This is the Harmonic Mean of S₁ and S₂.
4. Consecutive Numbers:
* The average of an arithmetic progression (AP) is the middle term (if odd number of terms) or the average of the two middle terms (if even number of terms).
* The average of the first 'n' natural numbers is (n+1)/2.
* The average of the first 'n' odd numbers is 'n'.
* The average of the first 'n' even numbers is (n+1).

2.6 Applications of Averages

* Age Problems: Calculating average age of a group, or changes in average age due to new inclusions/exclusions.
* Cricket Averages: Batting average (total runs / total innings played) and bowling average (total runs conceded / total wickets taken).
* Temperature/Rainfall: Calculating average daily, weekly, or monthly temperatures/rainfall.
* Financial Analysis: Average stock prices, average returns on investment.
* Data Interpretation: Analyzing trends and making comparisons across different datasets.

Part 3: Interconnection of Ratios and Averages & Advanced Concepts



Many UPSC CSAT problems cleverly combine ratios and averages, requiring a holistic understanding of both.

3.1 Problems Combining Ratios and Averages

These problems often involve:

* Finding actual values from ratios and then calculating averages:
* Example: The ratio of salaries of A, B, C is 2:3:5. If their average salary is ₹20,000, find C's salary.
* Let salaries be 2x, 3x, 5x.
* Average = (2x+3x+5x)/3 = 10x/3.
* 10x/3 = 20,000 => x = 6,000.
C's salary = 5x = 5 6,000 = ₹30,000.
* Using averages to determine ratios or changes in ratios:
* Example: The average age of 30 students is 15 years. If the teacher's age is included, the average age becomes 16 years. Find the teacher's age.
Total age of 30 students = 30 15 = 450 years.
Total age of (30 students + 1 teacher) = 31 16 = 496 years.
* Teacher's age = 496 - 450 = 46 years.
* This can then be used to find ratios, e.g., ratio of teacher's age to average student age.
* Mixture and Alligation: This is a powerful technique that directly uses the concept of weighted average and ratios to solve problems involving mixing two or more ingredients with different prices/concentrations to get a mixture of a desired price/concentration.
* Rule of Alligation:
``
Cost of Cheaper (C) Cost of Dearer (D)
/
/
Mean Cost (M)
/
/
(D - M) (M - C)
``
* The ratio of the quantity of cheaper to dearer is (D-M) : (M-C).
* [Diagram: The standard alligation cross diagram showing C, D, M and the resulting ratio (D-M):(M-C).]

3.2 Data Interpretation (DI) Perspective

Ratios and Averages are indispensable tools for Data Interpretation questions in CSAT. Aspirants must be adept at:

* Calculating Ratios from Tables/Graphs: Finding male-to-female ratios, production ratios between years, expenditure-to-income ratios, etc.
* Calculating Averages from Tables/Graphs: Average sales over years, average production of different companies, average marks in a subject.
* Percentage Change and Ratios: Often, questions combine percentage increase/decrease with ratios.
* Comparing Averages/Ratios: Identifying trends, highest/lowest values, and making informed conclusions based on calculated ratios and averages.
* Approximation: For large numbers, quick approximation techniques for ratios and averages are crucial to save time.

3.3 Common Pitfalls and Tips for UPSC Aspirants

1. Unit Consistency: Always ensure quantities are in the same units before forming ratios or calculating averages.
2. "Average of Averages" Fallacy: The average of averages is generally NOT the overall average unless the number of observations in each group is the same. Always use the combined mean formula.
3. Reading Questions Carefully: Distinguish between "ratio of A to B" and "ratio of B to A". Understand what quantities are being averaged.
4. Simplification: Always simplify ratios to their lowest terms.
5. Algebraic Approach: For complex problems, setting up equations using variables (e.g., 2x, 3x for a ratio of 2:3) is often the most reliable method.
6. Mental Math and Estimation: Practice quick calculations for ratios and averages, especially for DI questions.
7. Practice Diverse Problems: Cover all types of applications – age, mixtures, partnerships, speed-time-distance, and data interpretation.
