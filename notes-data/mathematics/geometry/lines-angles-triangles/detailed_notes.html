Introduction to Basic Geometry



Geometry, derived from Greek words "geo" (earth) and "metron" (measurement), literally means "earth measurement." It deals with the properties and relations of points, lines, surfaces, solids, and higher dimensional analogs. Euclidean geometry, which forms the basis of this discussion, is named after the ancient Greek mathematician Euclid, whose treatise "Elements" laid the foundation for much of what we study today.

The building blocks of geometry are:
1. Point: A point is an exact location in space. It has no dimension (no length, breadth, or height). It is represented by a dot and usually denoted by a capital letter (e.g., Point A).
Imagine a tiny dot on a paper.*
2. Line: A line is a straight path that extends infinitely in both directions. It has no thickness. It is represented by a line with arrows on both ends and denoted by a small letter (e.g., line 'l') or by two points on it (e.g., line AB).
Imagine a perfectly straight, endless road.*
3. Plane: A plane is a flat surface that extends infinitely in all directions. It has length and breadth but no thickness. A plane can be uniquely determined by three non-collinear points.
Imagine a perfectly flat, endless sheet of paper.*

From these fundamental concepts, we build upon lines, angles, and triangles.

I. Lines: The Foundation of Geometric Shapes



A line is a one-dimensional figure, having length but no width. It is a set of points extending infinitely in opposite directions.

#### 1. Key Definitions Related to Lines:

* Line Segment: A part of a line that has two distinct endpoints. It has a definite length. Denoted as $overline{AB}$.
Diagram: A line with points A and B marked, and the segment between them highlighted.*
* Ray: A part of a line that has one endpoint and extends infinitely in one direction. Denoted as $ ec{AB}$ (starting at A, going towards B).
Diagram: A line starting at point A and extending with an arrow towards B.*
* Collinear Points: Three or more points that lie on the same straight line.
Diagram: Points P, Q, R all lying on a single straight line.*
* Non-collinear Points: Points that do not lie on the same straight line.
Diagram: Points X, Y, Z forming a triangle, not on a single line.*

#### 2. Types of Lines:

* Parallel Lines: Two lines in a plane that never intersect, no matter how far they are extended. The perpendicular distance between them always remains constant. Denoted as $l parallel m$.
Diagram: Two straight lines, side-by-side, never touching.*
High-Yield Concept:* The concept of parallel lines is fundamental to understanding angles formed by a transversal.
* Intersecting Lines: Two lines that cross each other at a single common point. This common point is called the point of intersection.
Diagram: Two lines crossing each other, forming an 'X'.*
* Perpendicular Lines: Two lines that intersect each other at a right angle (90 degrees). Denoted as $l perp m$.
Diagram: Two lines crossing at a perfect 'T' shape, with a square symbol indicating the 90-degree angle.*
* Concurrent Lines: Three or more lines in a plane that intersect at a single common point.
Diagram: Three or more lines all passing through the same central point.*

II. Angles: The Measure of Turn



An angle is formed when two rays share a common endpoint. The common endpoint is called the vertex, and the two rays are called the arms or sides of the angle.

#### 1. Measurement of Angles:

Angles are typically measured in degrees (°). A full circle is 360°.
Imagine a clock face, the hands forming different angles.*

#### 2. Types of Angles:

* Acute Angle: An angle whose measure is greater than 0° but less than 90°.
Diagram: An angle smaller than a right angle, e.g., 45°.*
* Right Angle: An angle whose measure is exactly 90°. It is often denoted by a small square symbol at the vertex.
Diagram: An 'L' shape, with the square symbol at the corner.*
* Obtuse Angle: An angle whose measure is greater than 90° but less than 180°.
Diagram: An angle wider than a right angle but not flat, e.g., 120°.*
* Straight Angle: An angle whose measure is exactly 180°. It forms a straight line.
Diagram: A straight line with a point on it, indicating the 180° angle.*
* Reflex Angle: An angle whose measure is greater than 180° but less than 360°.
Diagram: An angle where the arc goes 'outside' the two rays, showing a large turn, e.g., 270°.*
* Complete Angle (Full Angle): An angle whose measure is exactly 360°. It represents a full rotation.
Diagram: A point with a circle drawn around it, indicating a full rotation.*

#### 3. Pairs of Angles:

* Complementary Angles: Two angles whose sum is 90°. Each angle is the complement of the other.
Example: 30° and 60° are complementary.*
* Supplementary Angles: Two angles whose sum is 180°. Each angle is the supplement of the other.
Example: 70° and 110° are supplementary.*
* Adjacent Angles: Two angles that share a common vertex and a common arm, but no common interior points.
Diagram: Two angles side-by-side, sharing a ray in the middle.*
* Linear Pair of Angles: A pair of adjacent angles whose non-common arms are opposite rays (form a straight line). Their sum is always 180° (they are supplementary).
Diagram: A straight line with a ray extending from a point on it, forming two adjacent angles that sum to 180°.*
* Vertically Opposite Angles: When two lines intersect, they form two pairs of vertically opposite angles. These angles are always equal.
Diagram: Two intersecting lines forming an 'X'. The angles opposite each other are equal. If the top angle is A, the bottom angle is A. If the left angle is B, the right angle is B.*

#### 4. Angles Formed by a Transversal with Parallel Lines:

A transversal is a line that intersects two or more distinct lines at distinct points. When a transversal intersects two parallel lines, it creates several pairs of angles with specific relationships.

Diagram: Two parallel lines, $l$ and $m$, intersected by a transversal $t$. Label the 8 angles formed (1 to 8).*

Let's assume lines $l$ and $m$ are parallel, and $t$ is the transversal.
* Corresponding Angles: Angles in the same relative position at each intersection. They are equal.
Pairs: (Angle 1 & Angle 5), (Angle 2 & Angle 6), (Angle 3 & Angle 7), (Angle 4 & Angle 8).*
High-Yield Concept:* This is the fundamental property from which other angle relationships are derived.
* Alternate Interior Angles: Angles on opposite sides of the transversal and between the parallel lines. They are equal.
Pairs: (Angle 3 & Angle 6), (Angle 4 & Angle 5).*
* Alternate Exterior Angles: Angles on opposite sides of the transversal and outside the parallel lines. They are equal.
Pairs: (Angle 1 & Angle 8), (Angle 2 & Angle 7).*
* Consecutive Interior Angles (Co-interior or Allied Angles): Angles on the same side of the transversal and between the parallel lines. They are supplementary (sum to 180°).
Pairs: (Angle 3 & Angle 5), (Angle 4 & Angle 6).*
* Consecutive Exterior Angles: Angles on the same side of the transversal and outside the parallel lines. They are supplementary (sum to 180°).
Pairs: (Angle 1 & Angle 7), (Angle 2 & Angle 8).*

* Converse of these theorems: If any of these conditions (e.g., corresponding angles are equal) hold true, then the two lines intersected by the transversal must be parallel. This is crucial for proving lines parallel.

III. Triangles: The Simplest Polygon



A triangle is a polygon with three sides and three vertices. It is the simplest closed figure in Euclidean geometry.

#### 1. Basic Definitions:

* Vertices: The three points where the sides meet (e.g., A, B, C).
* Sides: The three line segments connecting the vertices (e.g., AB, BC, CA).
* Angles: The three interior angles formed by the sides at the vertices (e.g., $angle A, angle B, angle C$).

#### 2. Classification of Triangles:

Triangles can be classified based on the length of their sides or the measure of their angles.

A. By Sides:
* Scalene Triangle: A triangle in which all three sides have different lengths. Consequently, all three angles also have different measures.
Diagram: A triangle with visibly unequal sides.*
* Isosceles Triangle: A triangle in which two sides are of equal length. The angles opposite to the equal sides are also equal (base angles).
Diagram: A triangle with two sides marked as equal, and the two base angles marked as equal.*
Properties:* The altitude from the vertex angle to the base bisects the base and the vertex angle.
* Equilateral Triangle: A triangle in which all three sides are of equal length. Consequently, all three angles are also equal, each measuring 60°.
Diagram: A triangle with all three sides marked as equal, and all three angles marked as 60°.*
Properties:* An equilateral triangle is also equiangular. All altitudes, medians, and angle bisectors are of equal length and coincide.

B. By Angles:
* Acute-angled Triangle: A triangle in which all three interior angles are acute (less than 90°).
Diagram: A triangle where all angles are clearly less than 90°.*
* Right-angled Triangle: A triangle in which one of the interior angles is a right angle (exactly 90°). The side opposite the right angle is called the hypotenuse (always the longest side). The other two sides are called legs.
Diagram: A triangle with one angle marked as 90°.*
High-Yield Concept:* Pythagorean Theorem: In a right-angled triangle, the square of the hypotenuse (c) is equal to the sum of the squares of the other two sides (a and b): $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$.
Pythagorean Triplets:* Sets of three positive integers a, b, and c, such that $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$. Common triplets: (3, 4, 5), (5, 12, 13), (8, 15, 17), (7, 24, 25).
* Obtuse-angled Triangle: A triangle in which one of the interior angles is obtuse (greater than 90°).
Diagram: A triangle with one angle clearly greater than 90°.*

#### 3. Properties of Triangles:

* Angle Sum Property: The sum of the interior angles of any triangle is always 180°.
Theorem: In $ riangle ABC$, $angle A + angle B + angle C = 180^circ$.*
Proof Idea: Draw a line parallel to one side through the opposite vertex and use alternate interior angles.*
* Exterior Angle Property: If a side of a triangle is produced, then the exterior angle so formed is equal to the sum of the two opposite interior angles.
Diagram: A triangle ABC, with side BC extended to D. $angle ACD$ is the exterior angle. $angle ACD = angle A + angle B$.*
* Triangle Inequality Theorem: The sum of the lengths of any two sides of a triangle must be greater than the length of the third side. Also, the difference between the lengths of any two sides must be less than the length of the third side.
For sides a, b, c: $a+b > c$, $b+c > a$, $c+a > b$. And $|a-b| < c$, $|b-c| < a$, $|c-a| < b$.*
High-Yield Concept:* This property helps determine if a given set of three lengths can form a triangle.
* Relationship between Sides and Angles: In any triangle, the side opposite the larger angle is longer, and conversely, the angle opposite the longer side is larger.
Example: If $angle A > angle B$, then side BC > side AC.*

#### 4. Special Lines and Points in a Triangle:

* Median: A line segment joining a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side. A triangle has three medians.
Diagram: A triangle with a line from a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side.*
* Centroid: The point of intersection of the three medians. It divides each median in the ratio 2:1 (from the vertex to the midpoint). The centroid is the center of gravity of the triangle.
* Altitude (Height): A line segment from a vertex perpendicular to the opposite side (or its extension). A triangle has three altitudes.
Diagram: A triangle with a line from a vertex perpendicular to the opposite side.*
* Orthocenter: The point of intersection of the three altitudes. Its position varies: inside for acute, at the vertex of the right angle for right-angled, outside for obtuse.
* Angle Bisector: A line segment that divides an angle into two equal angles. A triangle has three angle bisectors.
Diagram: A triangle with a line bisecting one of its angles.*
* Incenter: The point of intersection of the three angle bisectors. It is equidistant from all three sides of the triangle and is the center of the incircle (the largest circle that can be inscribed inside the triangle).
* Perpendicular Bisector: A line that is perpendicular to a side and passes through its midpoint. A triangle has three perpendicular bisectors.
Diagram: A triangle with a line perpendicular to a side and passing through its midpoint.*
* Circumcenter: The point of intersection of the three perpendicular bisectors. It is equidistant from all three vertices of the triangle and is the center of the circumcircle (the circle that passes through all three vertices of the triangle).

#### 5. Congruence of Triangles:

Two geometric figures are congruent if they have the same shape and the same size. For triangles, this means all corresponding sides and all corresponding angles are equal.
Notation: $ riangle ABC cong riangle PQR$*

Criteria for Congruence:
* SSS (Side-Side-Side): If three sides of one triangle are equal to the three corresponding sides of another triangle, then the triangles are congruent.
* SAS (Side-Angle-Side): If two sides and the included angle (the angle between the two sides) of one triangle are equal to the corresponding two sides and the included angle of another triangle, then the triangles are congruent.
* ASA (Angle-Side-Angle): If two angles and the included side (the side between the two angles) of one triangle are equal to the corresponding two angles and the included side of another triangle, then the triangles are congruent.
* AAS (Angle-Angle-Side): If two angles and a non-included side of one triangle are equal to the corresponding two angles and the non-included side of another triangle, then the triangles are congruent. (This is often considered a corollary of ASA).
* RHS (Right-angle-Hypotenuse-Side): If in two right-angled triangles, the hypotenuse and one side of one triangle are equal to the hypotenuse and one side of the other triangle, then the triangles are congruent.

#### 6. Similarity of Triangles:

Two geometric figures are similar if they have the same shape but not necessarily the same size. For triangles, this means their corresponding angles are equal, and their corresponding sides are proportional.
Notation: $ riangle ABC sim riangle PQR$*

Criteria for Similarity:
* AA (Angle-Angle) or AAA (Angle-Angle-Angle): If two angles (and thus all three) of one triangle are respectively equal to two angles of another triangle, then the triangles are similar.
* SSS (Side-Side-Side) Similarity: If the ratios of the corresponding sides of two triangles are equal, then the triangles are similar.
i.e., $ rac{AB}{PQ} = rac{BC}{QR} = rac{CA}{RP}$*
* SAS (Side-Angle-Side) Similarity: If one angle of a triangle is equal to one angle of another triangle and the sides including these angles are proportional, then the triangles are similar.
i.e., $angle A = angle P$ and $ rac{AB}{PQ} = rac{AC}{PR}$*

Key Theorems related to Similarity:
* Basic Proportionality Theorem (BPT) or Thales Theorem: If a line is drawn parallel to one side of a triangle intersecting the other two sides in distinct points, then the other two sides are divided in the same ratio.
Diagram: $ riangle ABC$ with line DE parallel to BC, intersecting AB at D and AC at E. Then $ rac{AD}{DB} = rac{AE}{EC}$.*
* Converse of BPT: If a line divides any two sides of a triangle in the same ratio, then the line is parallel to the third side.
* Area Ratio of Similar Triangles: The ratio of the areas of two similar triangles is equal to the square of the ratio of their corresponding sides.
If $ riangle ABC sim riangle PQR$, then $ rac{ ext{Area}( riangle ABC)}{ ext{Area}( riangle PQR)} = left( rac{AB}{PQ} ight)^2 = left( rac{BC}{QR} ight)^2 = left( rac{CA}{RP} ight)^2$.*

#### 7. Area of a Triangle:

* Using Base and Height: Area $= rac{1}{2} imes ext{base} imes ext{height}$. This is the most common formula.
Diagram: A triangle with its base and perpendicular height clearly marked.*
* Heron's Formula: For a triangle with sides a, b, c, let $s$ be the semi-perimeter ($s = rac{a+b+c}{2}$).
* Area $= sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}$. This is useful when only side lengths are known.
* Using Trigonometry (Advanced, but good for GK awareness): Area $= rac{1}{2} ab sin C$, where a and b are two sides and C is the included angle.

#### 8. Special Right Triangles:

* 45-45-90 Triangle (Isosceles Right Triangle): The ratio of sides is $1:1:sqrt{2}$. If legs are 'x', hypotenuse is $xsqrt{2}$.
Diagram: A right triangle with two equal angles (45°) and side ratios.*
* 30-60-90 Triangle: The ratio of sides is $1:sqrt{3}:2$. If the side opposite 30° is 'x', then the side opposite 60° is $xsqrt{3}$, and the hypotenuse is $2x$.
Diagram: A right triangle with angles 30°, 60°, 90° and side ratios.*

High-Yield Concepts for UPSC Aspirants:



1. Parallel Lines and Transversals: Master the relationships between angles (corresponding, alternate interior/exterior, consecutive interior/exterior). These are frequently tested. Remember the converses to prove lines parallel.
2. Angle Sum and Exterior Angle Properties of Triangles: These are fundamental for solving many problems involving unknown angles.
3. Pythagorean Theorem and Triplets: Essential for right-angled triangles. Memorize common triplets (3,4,5; 5,12,13 etc.) to save time.
4. Triangle Inequality Theorem: Crucial for determining the feasibility of forming a triangle with given side lengths.
5. Special Points in a Triangle (Centroid, Orthocenter, Incenter, Circumcenter): Understand their definitions, properties, and how they relate to medians, altitudes, angle bisectors, and perpendicular bisectors. Their locations (inside/outside/on vertex) for different types of triangles are also important.
6. Congruence vs. Similarity: Clearly differentiate between these two concepts. Congruence is about identical shape and size, while similarity is about identical shape but proportional size.
7. Basic Proportionality Theorem (BPT): A very important theorem for problems involving parallel lines within a triangle and ratios of sides.
8. Area Formulas: Know when to apply $ rac{1}{2} imes ext{base} imes ext{height}$ and Heron's formula.
9. Visualisation: Practice drawing diagrams for every problem. This helps in understanding the relationships and identifying the correct theorems to apply.
10. Problem-Solving Strategy: For geometry problems, always:
* Draw a clear diagram.
* Label all given information.
* Identify what needs to be found.
* Recall relevant theorems and properties.
* Work step-by-step.
