### **1. Introduction to Number Systems**
*   **Definition:** A number system is a framework used to represent and categorize numbers, enabling mathematical operations and comparisons. It forms the bedrock of mathematics.
*   **Purpose:** To simplify the understanding, manipulation, and classification of numerical values encountered in various mathematical contexts.

### **2. Classification of Numbers**
Numbers are broadly classified into sets, often visualized as nested categories.

#### **2.1. Complex Numbers ($\mathbb{C}$)**
*   **Definition:** The broadest set of numbers, comprising both Real and Imaginary numbers.
*   **Form:** $a + bi$, where $a$ and $b$ are real numbers, and $i = \sqrt{-1}$ (the imaginary unit).
*   **Relevance:** Essential in advanced algebra, physics, and engineering.

#### **2.2. Real Numbers ($\mathbb{R}$)**
*   **Definition:** All numbers that can be plotted on a continuous number line.
*   Includes all rational and irrational numbers.
*   **Sub-classifications:**

    *   ##### **2.2.1. Rational Numbers ($\mathbb{Q}$)**
        *   **Definition:** Numbers that can be expressed as a fraction $p/q$, where $p, q$ are integers and $q \neq 0$.
        *   **Decimal Representation:** Terminating (e.g., 0.25, 1.5) or Non-terminating **repeating** decimals (e.g., $0.333..., 1.2323...$).
        *   **Examples:** $1/4, -3/7, 8, 0, 5.75, \sqrt{16}$
        *   **Sub-classifications:**

            *   ###### **2.2.1.1. Integers ($\mathbb{Z}$)**
                *   **Definition:** Whole numbers (positive, negative, and zero) without fractional or decimal parts.
                *   **Examples:** ..., -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3, ...
                *   **Sub-classifications:**

                    *   ####### **2.2.1.1.1. Whole Numbers ($\mathbb{W}$)**
                        *   **Definition:** All natural numbers including zero.
                        *   **Examples:** 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, ...

                    *   ####### **2.2.1.1.2. Natural Numbers ($\mathbb{N}$)** (or Counting Numbers)
                        *   **Definition:** Positive integers starting from 1.
                        *   **Examples:** 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, ...

            *   ###### **2.2.1.2. Fractions**
                *   **Definition:** Numbers representing parts of a whole or ratios of integers (e.g., $2/3$).
                *   **Types:** Proper (Numerator < Denominator, e.g., $1/2$), Improper (Numerator > Denominator, e.g., $7/4$), Mixed (Whole number + Proper fraction, e.g., $2 \frac{1}{3}$).

    *   ##### **2.2.2. Irrational Numbers ($\mathbb{I}$ or $\mathbb{Q}'$)**
        *   **Definition:** Real numbers that **cannot** be expressed in the form $p/q$.
        *   **Decimal Representation:** Non-terminating **non-repeating** decimals.
        *   **Examples:** $\pi \approx 3.14159..., \sqrt{2} \approx 1.41421..., e \approx 2.71828...$
        *   **Key Properties:**
            *   Sum/difference of a rational and an irrational number is irrational.
            *   Product/quotient of a non-zero rational and an irrational number is irrational.

### **3. Properties of Real Numbers**
These fundamental properties govern how real numbers interact under addition and multiplication.

#### **3.1. Commutative Property**
*   **Addition:** The order of numbers does not affect the sum. $a + b = b + a$. (e.g., $7 + 2 = 2 + 7 = 9$)
*   **Multiplication:** The order of numbers does not affect the product. $a \times b = b \times a$. (e.g., $7 \times 2 = 2 \times 7 = 14$)

#### **3.2. Associative Property**
*   **Addition:** The grouping of numbers does not affect the sum. $(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)$. (e.g., $(1+5)+3 = 1+(5+3) = 9$)
*   **Multiplication:** The grouping of numbers does not affect the product. $(a \times b) \times c = a \times (b \times c)$. (e.g., $(1 \times 5) \times 3 = 1 \times (5 \times 3) = 15$)

#### **3.3. Distributive Property**
*   Multiplication distributes over addition. $a \times (b + c) = (a \times b) + (a \times c)$. (e.g., $4 \times (2+3) = (4 \times 2) + (4 \times 3) = 8 + 12 = 20$)

#### **3.4. Identity Property**
*   **Additive Identity:** Adding zero to any number yields the number itself. $a + 0 = a$.
*   **Multiplicative Identity:** Multiplying any number by one yields the number itself. $a \times 1 = a$.

#### **3.5. Inverse Property**
*   **Additive Inverse:** For every number $a$, there exists an opposite number $-a$ such that their sum is zero. $a + (-a) = 0$.
*   **Multiplicative Inverse (Reciprocal):** For every non-zero number $a$, there exists a number $1/a$ such that their product is one. $a \times (1/a) = 1$.

### **4. Basic Operations on Numbers**
*   **Core Operations:** Addition (+), Subtraction (-), Multiplication (×), Division (÷).
*   **Order of Operations (BODMAS/PEMDAS):** A standard sequence for evaluating mathematical expressions.
    *   **B**rackets / **P**arentheses
    *   **O**rders / **E**xponents (powers, square roots)
    *   **D**ivision and **M**ultiplication (from left to right)
    *   **A**ddition and **S**ubtraction (from left to right)

### **5. Divisibility Rules**
Simple rules to quickly determine if an integer is divisible by another without performing the division.

*   **By 2:** The last digit is an even number (0, 2, 4, 6, 8).
*   **By 3:** The sum of its digits is divisible by 3.
*   **By 4:** The number formed by its last two digits is divisible by 4.
*   **By 5:** The last digit is 0 or 5.
*   **By 6:** The number is divisible by both 2 and 3.
*   **By 8:** The number formed by its last three digits is divisible by 8.
*   **By 9:** The sum of its digits is divisible by 9.
*   **By 10:** The last digit is 0.
*   **By 11:** The alternating sum of its digits is divisible by 11. (e.g., For 121: $1-2+1=0$, which is divisible by 11).

### **6. Prime & Composite Numbers**

#### **6.1. Prime Numbers**
*   **Definition:** A natural number greater than 1 that has exactly two distinct positive divisors: 1 and itself.
*   **Examples:** 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19, ...
*   **Key Fact:** 2 is the smallest and only even prime number.

#### **6.2. Composite Numbers**
*   **Definition:** A natural number greater than 1 that has more than two distinct positive divisors.
*   **Examples:** 4, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12, ...
*   **Key Fact:** The number 1 is unique; it is neither prime nor composite.

#### **6.3. Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic (Unique Prime Factorization Theorem)**
*   **Statement:** Every composite number can be uniquely expressed as a product of prime numbers, disregarding the order of the prime factors.
*   **Example:** $24 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 3 = 2^3 \times 3$. This factorization is unique.

### **7. Highest Common Factor (HCF) / Greatest Common Divisor (GCD)**
*   **Definition:** The largest positive integer that divides two or more integers without leaving a remainder.

*   **Methods to Find HCF:**
    *   **Prime Factorization Method:**
        1.  Find the prime factorization of each number.
        2.  Identify all common prime factors.
        3.  Multiply these common prime factors, each raised to the lowest power it appears in any of the factorizations.
        *   **Example:** HCF(30, 42)
            *   $30 = 2^1 \times 3^1 \times 5^1$
            *   $42 = 2^1 \times 3^1 \times 7^1$
            *   Common factors: $2^1, 3^1$. HCF = $2 \times 3 = 6$.
    *   **Euclidean Algorithm:** An efficient method for two integers.
        1.  Divide the larger number by the smaller number to get a remainder.
        2.  Replace the larger number with the smaller number, and the smaller number with the remainder.
        3.  Repeat until the remainder is 0. The last non-zero divisor is the HCF.
        *   **Example:** HCF(105, 42)
            *   $105 = 2 \times 42 + 21$
            *   $42 = 2 \times 21 + 0$
            *   HCF = 21.

### **8. Lowest Common Multiple (LCM)**
*   **Definition:** The smallest positive integer that is a multiple of two or more integers.

*   **Methods to Find LCM:**
    *   **Prime Factorization Method:**
        1.  Find the prime factorization of each number.
        2.  Take all prime factors (common and uncommon) from the factorizations.
        3.  Multiply these prime factors, each raised to the highest power it appears in any of the factorizations.
        *   **Example:** LCM(30, 42)
            *   $30 = 2^1 \times 3^1 \times 5^1$
            *   $42 = 2^1 \times 3^1 \times 7^1$
            *   All factors with highest powers: $2^1, 3^1, 5^1, 7^1$. LCM = $2 \times 3 \times 5 \times 7 = 210$.
    *   **Relationship between HCF and LCM:** For any two positive integers $a$ and $b$:
        *   **$a \times b = HCF(a, b) \times LCM(a, b)$**
        *   Using the example (30, 42): $30 \times 42 = 1260$. $HCF(30, 42) \times LCM(30, 42) = 6 \times 210 = 1260$.

### **9. Number Line and Absolute Value**

#### **9.1. Number Line Representation**
*   **Concept:** A graphical representation where every real number corresponds to a unique point on a straight line.
*   **Properties:**
    *   Numbers increase from left to right.
    *   Zero is the origin, separating positive and negative numbers.
    *   **Density Property:** Between any two distinct real numbers, there exist infinitely many other real numbers (both rational and irrational).

#### **9.2. Absolute Value**
*   **Definition:** The distance of a number from zero on the number line, always a non-negative value. It indicates magnitude without direction.
*   **Notation:** $|x|$
*   **Formula:**
    *   $|x| = x$, if $x \ge 0$
    *   $|x| = -x$, if $x < 0$
*   **Examples:** $|7| = 7$, $|-7| = 7$, $|0| = 0$.

### **10. Special Types of Numbers**
*   **Even Numbers:** Integers perfectly divisible by 2 (e.g., -6, 0, 4, 10). Represented as $2n$.
*   **Odd Numbers:** Integers not perfectly divisible by 2 (e.g., -5, 1, 3, 9). Represented as $2n+1$ or $2n-1$.
*   **Perfect Numbers:** A positive integer that equals the sum of its proper positive divisors (divisors excluding itself). (e.g., $6 = 1+2+3$, $28 = 1+2+4+7+14$).
*   **Triangular Numbers:** Numbers that can form a triangular array of points. The $n^{th}$ triangular number is $n(n+1)/2$. (e.g., 1, 3, 6, 10...).
*   **Square Numbers (Perfect Squares):** Integers that are the product of an integer with itself. (e.g., $1^2=1, 2^2=4, 3^2=9, \dots$).
*   **Fibonacci Numbers:** A sequence where each number is the sum of the two preceding ones, typically starting with 0 and 1. (e.g., 0, 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13...).

### **11. Exponents and Powers**
*   **Definition:** An exponent indicates how many times a base number is multiplied by itself.
*   **Notation:** $a^n$ (read as "a to the power of n"), where $a$ is the base and $n$ is the exponent.
*   **Key Laws of Exponents:**
    *   $a^m \times a^n = a^{m+n}$
    *   $a^m / a^n = a^{m-n}$ ($a \neq 0$)
    *   $(a^m)^n = a^{mn}$
    *   $(ab)^n = a^n b^n$
    *   $(a/b)^n = a^n / b^n$ ($b \neq 0$)
    *   $a^0 = 1$ ($a \neq 0$)
    *   $a^{-n} = 1/a^n$ ($a \neq 0$)

### **12. Radicals (Roots)**
*   **Definition:** The inverse operation of exponentiation, finding a number that, when multiplied by itself 'n' times, equals a given number.
*   **Notation:** $\sqrt[n]{x}$ (read as "the nth root of x").
*   **Relationship to Exponents:** $\sqrt[n]{x} = x^{1/n}$
*   **Common Roots:** Square root ($\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}$), Cube root ($\sqrt[3]{x} = x^{1/3}$).
