4. COMPLETE THEORY

The Indian freedom struggle is a tapestry woven from constitutional petitions, revolutionary fervour, and the moral force of Gandhian non‑violence. Understanding this mosaic requires a layered approach – first, the birth of the Indian National Congress (INC) as a forum for educated elites; second, the infusion of mass politics through Mahatma Gandhi’s leadership; and third, the interplay of parallel revolutionary streams that pressured the British Crown from all sides.

4.1 Genesis of the Indian National Congress (1885‑1905)

The INC was founded on 28 December 1885 in Surat by A.O. Hume, a retired British officer, with the aim of providing a “voice” for Indian aspirations. Early sessions (Bombay 1885, Calcutta 1886) were dominated by moderates such as Dadabhai Naoroji, Gopal Krishna Gokhale, and Surendranath Banerjee. Their strategy hinged on petitions, petitions, and petitions – a “constitutional method” that sought incremental reforms. The “Laws of the Land” (e.g., Indian Councils Act 1892) merely widened the legislative council, but the moderate’s reliance on the British sense of justice proved insufficient for radical change.

4.2 Rise of Extremism (1905‑1915)

The Partition of Bengal (1905) was the catalyst that split the INC into Moderates and Extremists. Leaders like Bal Gangadhar Tilak, Lala Lajpat Rai, and Bipin Chandra Pal championed “Swaraj” through Swadeshi, boycott of British goods, and direct action. Their slogan “Swaraj is my birthright” resonated with the masses, especially in Bengal, Punjab, and Maharashtra. The British responded with the Arrests and Sedition Acts, but the ferment continued, laying the groundwork for Gandhi’s entry.

4.3 Gandhi’s Entry and the Moral Revolution (1915‑1922)

Mahatma Gandhi returned from South Africa in 1915, armed with the philosophy of Satyagraha (truth‑force) and Ahimsa (non‑violence). His first Indian experiment was the Champaran Satyagraha (1917), where he organized peasants against the oppressive indigo planters. This success proved that disciplined non‑violent mass mobilisation could pressure the Crown without armed conflict.

The Non‑Cooperation Movement (1920‑22) was Gandhi’s first nationwide call to boycott British institutions – schools, courts, and goods. The movement was triggered by the Jallianwala Bagh massacre (1919) and the Khilafat agitation, creating a Hindu‑Muslim coalition. Although the movement was abruptly halted after the Chauri Chaura incident (1922), it cemented the idea that the entire Indian populace could be mobilized under a single moral banner.

4.4 The Inter‑War Period – Consolidation & Schisms (1923‑1939)

Post‑1922, the INC oscillated between constitutional negotiations (Simon Commission 1928) and mass agitation (Civil Disobedience 1930‑34). The Salt Satyagraha (1930) – the 240‑mile Dandi march – became a symbol of defiance against the salt tax, illustrating how a simple commodity could become a political weapon.

Parallel to Gandhi’s non‑violent path, revolutionary groups such as Hindustan Socialist Republican Association (HSRA) and the Indian National Army (INA) pursued armed struggle. Bhagat Singh’s martyrdom (1931) and Subhas Chandra Bose’s call for “Arise, Awake, and Unite” kept the idea of armed resistance alive, forcing the British to consider both political and security dimensions.

4.5 World War II & the Quit India Movement (1939‑1945)

World War II forced the British to involve India in the war effort without consulting Indian leaders. The Quit India Movement (1942) was a decisive, all‑out demand for immediate British exit. Though brutally suppressed, it demonstrated the impossibility of the “divide‑and‑rule” strategy; the British could no longer rely on a minority of loyalists.

In the same period, the INA under Subhas Bose, aided by Japan, fought alongside the Japanese in Burma and Northeast India. The INA’s trials (1945‑46) ignited massive public sympathy, compelling the British to recognise the political weight of the armed nationalist sentiment.

4.6 Towards Independence (1946‑1947)

The Cabinet Mission (1946) attempted to preserve a united India through a federal structure, but communal tensions escalated. The Direct Action Day (16 Aug 1946) and subsequent riots made partition inevitable. The INC, under Jawaharlal Nehru, negotiated the Indian Independence Act (1947), culminating in the birth of two sovereign states on 15 August 1947.

4.7 Legacy for Defence Services

The freedom struggle forged a tradition of disciplined mass mobilisation, strategic patience, and the concept of “total warfare” – a blend of political, economic, and military pressure. Modern Indian armed forces inherit this legacy: the emphasis on “hearts‑and‑minds” operations, civil‑military synergy in counter‑insurgency, and the strategic use of non‑kinetic tools (e.g., information warfare) echo Gandhi’s Satyagraha in a contemporary context.

8. MEMORY TRICKS

  • GANDHI = “GO AND HIT IN DEMOCRACY” – Remember his method: Go (march), And (non‑cooperate), Hit (strike salt), In (civil disobedience), Democracy (political reform).
  • INC = “I N‑CHARGE” – The party took charge of the freedom fight from 1885 to 1947.
  • NON‑COOP = “N O N C O O P” – Each letter stands for a boycott: N—National schools, O—Official courts, C—Clerks, O—Offices, O—Orders, P—Products.
  • SAALT = “Salt, Ahimsa, Ahara, Lathi, Tactics” – Salt Satyagraha’s core components for quick recall.
  • INA = “INdian Armour” – Visualise INA soldiers wearing armoured jackets, linking to modern Indian armoured corps.
  • HSRA = “HARD SOLDIER RAGE ACT” – Reminds that HSRA was a radical, angry group seeking immediate action.

10. PYQ ANALYSIS

Past five years of CDS, AFCAT and NDA papers show that the “Freedom Struggle” chapter contributes an average of 12‑15% of the total history marks. The most frequently asked sub‑topics are:

  • Chronology of major movements – dates, slogans, and outcomes (e.g., Non‑Cooperation, Civil Disobedience, Quit India).
  • Roles of key personalities – Gandhi, Tilak, Nehru, Subhas Bose, Bhagat Singh.
  • INC’s evolution – from a petitioning body to a mass party.
  • Impact of the freedom struggle on the formation of the Indian Armed Forces (e.g., INA’s influence on post‑1947 army ethos).

Question style ranges from straightforward MCQs to assertion‑reason and match‑the‑column. Recent trends (2023‑2025) show an increased focus on “linkage with defence” – e.g., questions asking how the INA experience shaped the Indian Navy’s early recruitment or how Gandhi’s civil‑resistance tactics influence modern counter‑insurgency doctrine.

11. EXAM PERSPECTIVE

CDS and AFCAT often ask for concise statements – “What was the significance of the Dandi March?” – and for analytical links – “How did the Quit India Movement affect the British military deployment in India?” NDA papers, however, love pattern‑type questions like “Match the leader with the movement” and “Identify the correct chronological order”. Understanding the cause‑effect chain and memorising dates will fetch full marks.

12. COMMON MISTAKES

  • Confusing the Non‑Cooperation (1920‑22) with the Civil Disobedience (1930‑34) – remember the salt‑tax trigger for the latter.
  • Attributing the “Quit India” slogan to Gandhi; it was actually coined by the INC Working Committee.
  • Claiming that the INA was formed after 1945 – it was raised in 1942.
  • Mixing up the dates of the Champaran (1917) and Kheda (1918) Satyagrahas – Champaran preceded Kheda.
  • Assuming Subhas Bose was a Congress President throughout the 1940s; he resigned in 1939.
  • Over‑generalising “Gandhi’s movement = non‑violence” – remember the occasional violent outbreaks (e.g., Chauri Chaura) that led him to halt the movement.
  • Ignoring the role of women (e.g., Sarojini Naidu, Aruna Asaf Ali) – exam setters often ask for “women’s participation”.
  • Mis‑stating the date of the Jallianwala Bagh massacre – it was 13 April 1919, not 1918.

13. MILITARY CONNECTION

  • INA’s guerrilla tactics in Burma laid early foundations for India’s special forces doctrine.
  • Gandhi’s emphasis on “mass mobilisation” resonates with modern “Hearts‑and‑Minds” operations in counter‑insurgency (e.g., Operation Rakshak).
  • Post‑1947, many INA veterans joined the Indian Army, influencing its early leadership ethos.
  • HSRA’s revolutionary propaganda inspired the Indian Air Force’s “Swami Vivekananda” motto of daring sacrifice.
  • The 1935 Government of India Act created provincial governments that later became the administrative backbone for defence logistics during the 1962 and 1971 wars.

14. CURRENT AFFAIRS INTEGRATION (2023‑2025)

  • 2024: Indian Army’s “Swadeshi Weapon Programme” echoes Gandhi’s Swadeshi principle by promoting indigenous arms.
  • 2023: The centenary of the Salt March was commemorated by the Ministry of Defence with a “Water Conservation” campaign, linking historic non‑violent protest to modern resource security.
  • 2025: Defence Ministry’s “INA Veterans Memorial” inaugurated in Kolkata, acknowledging the armed struggle’s contribution to the nation‑building narrative.
  • 2024: NDA cadets participated in a “Gandhi‑Jeevan‑Jal” symposium on non‑violent conflict resolution, showcasing the relevance of Satyagraha in modern peace‑keeping doctrines.
  • 2023: The “Quit India” anniversary was marked by a joint Indo‑US naval exercise on “Freedom of Navigation”, symbolising the continuation of the struggle for sovereign maritime rights.
