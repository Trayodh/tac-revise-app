4. COMPLETE THEORY

4.1. Foundations – Shivaji’s Early Years (1630‑1656)

Shivaji Bhonsle was born in 1630 at Shivneri Fort. His father, Shahaji, served the Deccan Sultanates, exposing young Shivaji to both Marathi and Persian court cultures. By age 12, Shivaji was already mastering horse‑riding, swordsmanship, and fort‑architecture. The pivotal moment came in 1645 when he captured Torna Fort – a daring raid that announced his intent to challenge the Adil Shahi authority.

4.2. Consolidation – From Fort‑Wars to State‑Building (1656‑1674)

Shivaji’s strategy hinged on “Guerilla (Ganimi) warfare”. He exploited the rugged Sahyadri terrain, using Maval foot soldiers adept at moving through mountain passes. The famous “Shiva‑Shakti” night raids on Pune and Junnar demonstrated the psychological edge of surprise attacks.

Administrative reforms were equally revolutionary. The Ashta Pradhan council (eight ministers) replaced the traditional hereditary bureaucracy, ensuring merit‑based appointments. Revenue reforms introduced the Chauth (25% levy) and Sardeshmukhi (10% levy) on territories that acknowledged Maratha suzerainty but remained under Mughal or Sultanate control.

4.3. Coronation – The Birth of the Maratha Empire (1674)

On 3 April 1674, Shivaji performed the coronation at Raigad, assuming the title Chhatrapati. The ceremony was a blend of Hindu Vedic rites and indigenous royal customs, reinforcing his claim to a sovereign “Swarajya”. The coronation also legitimised the Rajya (administrative) division into Sarkars (districts) and Parganas (sub‑districts).

4.4. The Peshwa Era – From Shivaji’s Grandson to Bajirao I (1707‑1740)

Shivaji’s death (1680) ushered in a period of succession struggles. By 1713, the title Peshwa (prime minister) became the de‑facto ruler. Peshwa Balaji Vishwanath (1713‑1720) restored fiscal stability through aggressive Chauth collection in the Deccan.

The real turning point arrived with Bajirao I (1720‑1740). His “quick‑strike cavalry” doctrine, based on speed, surprise, and a flexible command hierarchy, allowed the Marathas to march from Pune to Delhi in just three months (1737). Bajirao’s campaigns transformed the empire from a regional power into a pan‑Indian force, often termed the “‘Second Great Power of the 18th Century’”.

4.5. Military System – Structure & Technology

Component Key Features Modern Parallel
Infantry (Maval) Light, hill‑trained foot soldiers; expert in ambush and rock‑cut fort assaults. Mountain infantry (e.g., Indian Army’s 11 RR).
Cavalry (Bargi, Pindari) Fast, mobile shock troops; used for rapid raids, flanking, and revenue collection. Armoured reconnaissance units.
Artillery Adopted European cannon designs; emphasis on mobility (light field guns). Self‑propelled artillery.
Navy (Angre) Coastal forts, small frigates, and privateer tactics against European traders. Coastal patrol and littoral warfare.

Key tactical doctrines:

  • Ganimi Kava (Guerrilla Warfare) – Emphasised night raids, use of terrain, and rapid withdrawal.
  • Shakti‑Yuddha (Force‑Based War) – Adopted by Bajirao for open field battles, relying on heavy cavalry charges.
  • Chauth & Sardeshmukhi – Economic instruments that funded a standing army of ~120,000 at its peak.

4.6. Decline & Legacy (1761‑1818)

The disastrous Third Battle of Panipat (1761) crippled Maratha cavalry, leading to internal fragmentation. The British East India Company exploited this weakness, culminating in the Third Anglo‑Maratha War (1817‑1818). However, the Maratha military ethos survived in the Indian National Army’s “Swadeshi” spirit and in today’s “Mountain Warfare” doctrines.

4.7. Why It Matters for Defence Exams

Understanding the Maratha system helps aspirants draw parallels with modern concepts such as asymmetric warfare, rapid‑deployment forces, and revenue‑based logistics – all of which feature in the Indian Defence Services’ syllabus.

12. COMMON MISTAKES

  • Confusing Chauth with Jizya – Chauth was a revenue levy; Jizya was a poll tax on non‑Muslims.
  • Attributing the title “Peshwa” to Shivaji – It became prominent only after 1713.
  • Mixing up Angre’s navy with the British East India Company fleet – Angre fought against them, not for them.
  • Stating that the Marathas defeated the Mughals at Panipat – Panipat was a defeat for the Marathas.
  • Assuming “Bargis” were a separate ethnic group – They were a cavalry unit, not a tribe.
  • Claiming Shivaji’s empire covered the entire Indian sub‑continent – It was primarily Deccan and parts of central India at his death.
  • Mixing the dates of Shivaji’s coronation (1674) with his death (1680) – Keep them distinct.
  • Confusing the “Mahar” (Mahar Regiment) of modern Indian Army with Maratha “Maval” infantry – Different eras.
