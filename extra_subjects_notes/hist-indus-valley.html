4. Complete Theory

4.1 Genesis & Chronology

The Indus Valley Civilization (IVC), often called the Harappan Civilization, blossomed around 3300 BC in the fertile floodplains of the Ghaggar‑Hakra (former Saraswati) river system. Archaeologists divide it into three phases:

  • Early Harappan (3300‑2600 BC): village‑scale settlements (e.g., Kot Diji) with nascent mud‑brick architecture.
  • Mature Harappan (2600‑1900 BC): urban apex – 12 major towns, sophisticated civic amenities.
  • Late Harappan (1900‑1300 BC): de‑urbanisation, spread to Gujarat, Punjab and even into the Ganges basin.

4.2 Major Sites & Their Strategic Placement

Site Location Key Feature
Harappa Punjab (Pakistan) Proto‑urban citadel, extensive granaries.
Mohenjo‑Daro Sindh (Pakistan) Great Bath, uniform brickwork, advanced drainage.
Dholavira Kutch (Gujarat, India) Water‑storage reservoirs, massive city walls.
Rakhigarhi Haryana (India) Largest known IVC settlement; strategic link between north‑west and north‑central India.

Strategically, these sites lie along ancient river courses, providing fertile soils, water, and natural trade corridors — a classic example of “geostrategic siting” that modern militaries still emulate when selecting forward bases.

4.3 Urban Planning & Architecture

Every major IVC city follows a remarkably uniform blueprint:

  • Grid layout: streets intersect at right angles, forming blocks of 30‑40 m per side.
  • Standardised bricks: the 1:2:4 ratio (e.g., 7 × 14 × 28 cm) allowed rapid construction and easy repair – akin to modern modular building kits.
  • Citadel & Lower Town: the citadel (often on a raised mound) housed administrative buildings, granaries, and possibly the ruler’s residence; the lower town accommodated artisans and merchants.
  • Drainage system: covered brick‑lined drains, manholes every 50 m, and soak‑away pits – arguably the world’s first municipal sanitation network.
  • Water‑storage: large rectangular reservoirs (e.g., Dholavira’s “Great Reservoir”) and the iconic Great Bath at Mohenjo‑Daro, indicating public hygiene and ritual use.

4.4 Economy & Trade

The IVC’s prosperity stemmed from a mixed agrarian‑craft‑trade economy.

Sector Key Products Evidence
Agriculture Wheat, barley, millets, cotton Carbonised grain remains, plough‑tips, cotton fibers.
Crafts Beadwork (carnelian, lapis), pottery, metallurgy (copper‑bronze) Thousands of seals, crucibles, moulds.
Trade Lapis lazuli (Afghanistan), tin (Iran), gold (Mesopotamia) Seals with Mesopotamian motifs, foreign raw materials.

Standardised weights (ranging from 1 kg to 100 g) and the presence of “seal‑impressions” suggest a sophisticated accounting system, comparable to today’s RFID‑based supply‑chain tracking.

4.5 Script, Seals & Language

The Indus script comprises about 400 distinct signs, mostly pictographic. Despite intensive attempts, it remains undeciphered due to the lack of a bilingual inscription (the “Rosetta Stone” is missing). Seals—often made of steatite—bear these signs alongside animal motifs, functioning as identity cards, trade labels, or administrative stamps.

4.6 Social & Political Structure

Unlike the towering palaces of Egypt or the ziggurats of Mesopotamia, the IVC leaves no obvious “royal” architecture. This has led scholars to propose a relatively egalitarian city‑state model:

  • Uniform housing sizes hint at limited wealth disparity.
  • Absence of weapons in domestic contexts suggests a society where force was minimal or centralized.
  • Public works (baths, drains) imply collective decision‑making—perhaps a council of elders or merchant guilds.

4.7 Decline Theories

From around 1900 BC, major sites show signs of abandonment. Main hypotheses:

  1. Climatic change: Monsoon weakening and the drying of the Ghaggar‑Hakra forced agrarian collapse.
  2. River migration: The Saraswati likely shifted eastward, leaving settlements water‑starved.
  3. Trade disruption: Decline of Mesopotamian demand reduced external cash flow.
  4. Possible invasions: Some suggest Aryan migration, but archaeological evidence is inconclusive.

Modern military planners study such “environment‑driven collapses” to anticipate supply‑line vulnerabilities in arid theatres.

4.8 Legacy

The IVC bequeaths us:

  • Urban sanitation concepts still taught in civil‑engineering curricula.
  • Standardisation of bricks and weights—early “quality‑control” practices.
  • Early evidence of municipal governance, relevant to modern peace‑keeping and nation‑building missions.

8. Memory Tricks

  • “HARD‑BAT” – Harappa, Aqueduct (Great Bath), Roads (grid), Drainage, Brick‑standard, Administration (citadel), Trade seals.
  • “UNiCORN” – The most common seal animal = unicorn, reminds you of unique script.
  • “SAND‑FORCE” – Imagine a modern army’s “sand‑force” building a base: first lay bricks (1:2:4), then dig drainage – same steps as IVC.
  • “RIVER‑HOP” – Ghaggar → “River Hop” – think of a hop‑scotch game where the ball (civilisation) moves from one river to another, then stops when the river dries.
  • “MOLAR‑MILL” – “Mohenjo‑Daro” sounds like “Molar Mill” – a massive grinding stone (Great Bath) used to grind out hygiene.

9. Important Facts (Exam Favourites)

  1. IVC covered ~1.2 million km², larger than modern-day Egypt.
  2. Standard brick size = 7 × 14 × 28 cm (1:2:4).
  3. Great Bath measured approx. 12 × 7 × 2.4 m.
  4. Harappa was first discovered in 1921 by Sir John Marshall.
  5. ~400 distinct signs in the Indus script; longest inscription = 34 signs.
  6. Granaries could store up to 12,000 tons of grain.
  7. Evidence of cotton spinning dates back to 5000 BC in the IVC.
  8. Seals often depict a unicorn – the most frequent motif.
  9. Urban population of Mohenjo‑Daro estimated at 35,000‑40,000.
  10. Drainage channels slope at 1 m per 100 m – a precision engineering feat.
  11. Standardised weight sets (1 kg, 500 g, 250 g, 100 g) found at many sites.
  12. Ivory, bronze, and shell ornaments indicate social stratification.
  13. Trade links extended to Mesopotamia (≈200 km via sea routes).
  14. Rakhigarhi’s excavations revealed a possible “royal” burial pit.
  15. Late Harappan sites show increased use of mud‑brick walls rather than baked bricks.
  16. Earliest evidence of urban sanitation predates Roman systems by 2,000 years.
  17. IVC’s “fire altars” are absent; religious practice remains speculative.
  18. Population decline coincides with the 4.2 ka climate event.
  19. Recent DNA studies suggest continuity with present‑day South Asians.
  20. “Dholavira” means “the city of the sea‑water” – its reservoirs still hold water after 4,000 years.

12. Common Mistakes & Corrections

Mistake Correction
Confusing Harappa with “Harappan script”. Harappa = site; script = “Indus script”.
Assuming IVC had pyramids. No pyramidal structures; focus on baths and granaries.
Attributing the “unicorn” motif to mythology. It is a common seal animal, not a mythological creature.
Stating the IVC declined due solely to “Aryan invasion”. Modern scholarship favours climate‑change and trade decline; invasions remain speculative.
Mixing up “Dholavira” with “Harappa” for reservoir facts. Dholavira is famed for its massive reservoirs; Harappa’s fame lies in granaries.
