Script and Language

  • The script and language are undeciphered.
  • The script is pictographic in nature.
  • The fish symbol is the most represented.
  • Overlapping of letters indicates writing from right to left in the first line and then left to right in the second line. This style is called boustrophedon.
  • A signboard inscription bearing 10 pictographs has been found from Dholavira in Gujarat.

Contributions of Harappan Civilisation

  • Flourished between 2600 BC and 1900 BC.
  • Major contributions include:
    • Standardised weights and measures.
    • Planned city construction.
    • Public buildings like the Great Bath.
    • Worship of fire, Mother goddess, tree, and phallus, which was carried forward by the Aryans.
    • Agricultural practices.
    • Use of seals for trade.
    • Ornaments and dresses.
  • The impact of Harappan civilisation could be seen on the socio, economic, political, and religious life of the people.

The Vedic Age

The history of the Vedic Age is largely known through its religious texts, the Vedas, which gave the period its name. The Vedas recorded not only the religion but also details of the political, social, and economic patterns of the Vedic people.

The Aryans

  • The word 'Aryan' is derived from 'Arya', meaning 'favourably disposed towards new corners', and later implied 'high birth'.
  • Generally, 'Aryan' refers to a language and not a race.
  • The most accepted view regarding their origin is that they lived in the great steppe land stretching from Poland to Central Asia.
  • They were semi-nomadic people.
  • In the 2nd millennium BC, they started moving from their original home, migrating Eastwards and Southwards.
  • The Boghaz Koi inscriptions (dated 1400 BC) provide information about a peace treaty between the Hittites and the Mittanis rulers.
  • These inscriptions mention the names of Vedic Gods: Indra, Mitra, Nasatya, and Varuna, supporting the Central Asian homeland theory for Aryans.
  • The Vedic Age is divided into two parts: Early Vedic or Rigvedic Age and Later Vedic Age.

Early Vedic or Rigvedic Age

  • The main source of information for this period is the Rigveda.
  • The founders of the Vedic culture were the Aryans, probably immigrant people whose first arrival in India is dated around 2000-1500 BC.
  • The Rig Veda states that Aryans first settled in the region called 'Sapta Sindhu' or the 'land of seven rivers'.
  • This region corresponds to present-day East Afghanistan, Punjab, and West Uttar Pradesh, encompassing the rivers Indus, Jhelum, Ravi, Chenab, Beas, Sutlej, and Saraswati.

The Rig Vedic Economy

  • Early Aryans were semi-nomadic and later became cultivators.
  • They gave great preference to cattle; hence, the ruling class consisted of warriors who fought for cattle.
  • 'Cow' was particularly preferred among cattle.
  • The word 'Gauri' (cow) is mentioned 176 times in the Rig Veda.
  • Terms related to 'Gau' include 'Gavishti' (search for cows) and 'Godhuli' (for daughter).
  • The term 'Aghanya' (not to be killed) has been used for cows.

The Rig Vedic Society

  • The Rig Vedic society was based on kinship.
  • Early Aryans were essentially tribal and egalitarian.
  • A tribe was called 'Jana'.
  • Society was patriarchal, with a preference for male children (who fought for cattle).
  • Existence of a joint family pattern.
  • The eldest male member of the family was known as 'Kulapa'.
  • 'Varna' was the term used for colour, suggesting that Aryan language speakers were fair and indigenous inhabitants were dark.
  • No evidence of a caste system is found; members of the same family indulged in different occupations, as illustrated in a hymn of the 9th mandala.
  • Non-Aryans were called Dasas or Shudras.

Teachings of Buddha

  • Four Noble Truths (Arya Satyas)

    • The world is full of sorrows (Sabbam Dukham).
    • The cause of sorrow is desire (Dwadash Nidan/Pratitya Samutpada).
    • If desires are conquered, all sorrows can be removed (Nirvana).
    • This can be achieved by following the Eight-Fold Path (Ashtangika Marga).
    • The second truth is based on Buddha's doctrine of Paticheha samutpada or Pratitya samutpada, which is the law of dependent origination or causation.

  • Eight-Fold Path (Ashtangika Marga)

    1. Right understanding
    2. Right thought
    3. Right speech
    4. Right action
    5. Right livelihood
    6. Right effort
    7. Right mindfulness
    8. Right concentration

  • Three Jewels (Triratnas)

    1. Buddha (the enlightened)
    2. Dhamma (doctrine)
    3. Sangha (order)

  • Code of Conduct

    1. Do not convert the property of others
    2. Do not commit violence
    3. Do not use intoxicants
    4. Do not speak a lie
    5. Do not indulge in corrupt practices

  • Key Beliefs

    • Nirvana: Also known as moksha or salvation. It refers to the concept of ultimate bliss, whereby a person gets freedom from the cycle of birth and death.
    • Ahimsa: Non-violence.
    • Law of Karma: Actions and their consequences.
    • Madhya Marga/Madhyama Pratipada: The middle path.

  • Terminology

    • Kutagorshala: A place where Buddhist monks discuss religious issues.

Division of Buddhism

Buddhism was divided into the following sects:

  • Hinayana or Lesser Wheel:
    • Followers believed in the original teachings of Buddha.
    • Believed in individual salvation.
    • Did not believe in idol-worship.
  • Mahayana or Greater Wheel:
    • Followers believed in the heavenliness of Buddha.
    • Sought salvation through the grace and help of Buddha and Bodhisattvas.
    • Came into existence during the reign of Kanishka.
  • Vajrayana or Vehicle of Thunderbolt:
    • Followers believed that salvation could be attained by acquiring magical powers (Vajra).
    • The chief divinities of this sect were Taras.
    • Vajrayana became popular in the Eastern part of India, particularly in Bengal and Bihar.

Buddhist Texts

  • Tripitakas (Buddhist Scriptures)

    • Vinaya Pitaka: Contains the rules and regulations of monastic discipline for monks. Also includes an account of the life and teaching of the Buddha.
    • Sutta Pitaka: Contains discourses delivered by Buddhist scholars like Sariputta, Ananda, and others. It lays down the principles of Buddhism and is a collection of Buddha's sermons, divided into five groups.
    • Abhidhamma Pitaka: Contains the philosophy of Buddha's teachings, investigating mind and matter to help understand things as they truly are.
  • Jatak Stories: Narrates about 550 previous births of Buddha.
  • Milindapanho: A conversation between the Greco-Bactrian king Menander and the Buddhist monk Nagasena, written in a question-and-answer format.
  • Kathavastu: A Hinayana granth, it is noted as the first book written in Sanskrit language in Buddhist religion.
  • Mahavibhashashastra: Written by Vasumitra, it is considered an encyclopedia of the Buddhist religion.
  • Dipavamsha and Mahavamsha: Written in Pali language, these texts provide historical accounts of Sri Lanka.

Buddhist Councils

  • The First Council (483 BC)

    • Location: Sattapanni (Saptaparni) cave near Rajagriha.
    • Chairman: Mahakassapa.
    • Key Events: Vinaya Pitaka was compiled by Upali, and Sutta Pitaka was compiled by Ananda.
    • King: Ajatashatru.

  • The Second Council (383 BC)

    • Location: Vaishali.
    • King: Kalashoka of the Shishunaga dynasty.
    • Chairman: Sabakami.
    • Key Events: Monks of Vaishali sought changes in rites, leading to the division of the sect into Sthavirvadins and Mahasamghikas.

  • The Third Council (250 BC)

    • Location: Pataliputra.
    • King: Ashoka (the Mauryan ruler).
    • President: Mogaliputta Tissa.
    • Purpose: To revise the Buddhist texts.

  • The Fourth Council (AD 72)

    • Location: Kashmir.
    • King: Kanishka (the Kushana ruler).
    • Presidents: Vasumitra and Ashwaghosha.
    • Key Events: Buddhism got divided into Hinayana and Mahayana.

Buddhist Architecture

  • Stupas: Doomed monuments containing relics of the Buddha. They are often carved with symbols of Buddha such as footprints, the Bodhi tree, and vases.
  • Viharas: Monastery halls, originally used by monks for study, solitary meditation, and communal worship.

Samudragupta (c. AD 335-375)

  • Advanced through the forest tract of Madhya Pradesh to the coast of Orissa and then moved up to Kanchi, the capital of Pallavas.
  • Some coins represent him as playing on the Veena.
  • Performed Asvamedha sacrifice.
  • Devotee of Vishnu and a follower of the Brahmanical religion.
  • Tolerant of other faiths; granted permission to the Buddhist king of Ceylon, Meghavarman, to build a monastery at Bodh Gaya.

Chandragupta II (AD 380-412) - Chandragupta Vikramaditya

  • Succeeded Samudragupta around AD 375.
  • Some historians place Ramagupta between Samudragupta and Chandragupta II.
  • The play Devichandraguptam of Visakhadatta mentions Ramagupta as the elder brother of Chandragupta II.
  • According to Devichandraguptam, Ramagupta agreed to surrender Queen Dhruvadevi to a Shaka ruler after Shakas attacked the Gupta Empire.
  • Chandragupta II objected, killed the Shaka chief, rescued Dhruvadevi, and later married her.
  • Gupta records do not refer to Ramagupta.
  • Followed a policy of political marriages: conciliated Naga chieftains by marrying Princess Kubernaga and allied with the Vakatakas of the Deccan by marrying his daughter Prabhavati to Rudrasena II.
  • First Gupta ruler to issue silver coins.
  • Adopted the titles Vikramaditya and Sakari in memory of his victory over the Shakas.
  • The Mehrauli iron pillar inscription near Qutub Minar enumerates the exploits of a king called Chandra, often identified with Chandragupta II.
  • The Chinese pilgrim Fa-Hein (399-414 AD) visited India during his reign.

Kumaragupta I (c. AD 415-455)

  • Adopted the title Mahendraditya.
  • Founded Nalanda University, a renowned university of ancient India.
  • Kalidas flourished during the reign of both Chandragupta II and Kumaragupta I.
  • Worshipper of Lord Kartikeya (son of Lord Shiva, whose vehicle is a peacock).

Skandagupta (c. AD 455-467) - Fall of Gupta Empire

  • Effectively faced the Hunas, fierce nomads from Central Asia.
  • Weak successors could not check the growing Huna power.
  • Feudatories rose in Bihar, Bengal, Madhya Pradesh, Vallabhi, etc., contributing to the decline of the empire.

Gupta Administration

  • Kings' Titles: Parameshwara, Maharajadhiraja, Parambhattaraka.
  • Issued the largest number of gold coins in Ancient India, which were called Dinars.
  • Kumaramatyas: Most important officers, appointed by the king in home provinces, mostly paid in cash.
  • Provincial Administration:
    • Empire divided into Bhuktis, each under the charge of an Uparika.
    • Bhuktis divided into districts (Vishyas), placed under a Vishyapati.
  • Village headman was very important.
  • Agraharas: Land grants gifted to Brahmanas by Gupta kings.

Gupta Social Development

  • Castes were further divided into sub-castes.
  • Practice of untouchability intensified, especially hatred for Chandalas.
  • Fa-hien mentions that the Chandalas lived outside the village and were distanced by the upper class.
  • Varna-Shankar became highlighted during this era.

Gupta Religion

  • The Bhagwad Gita was written during this period.
  • Buddhism declined.
  • Notable temples belonging to Gupta architecture: Vishnu temple at Deogarh (near Jhansi), a small temple near Sanchi, and a brick temple at Bhitargaon (near Kanpur).

Gupta Art and Crafts - The Golden Age

  • The Gupta period witnessed unprecedented activities and development in art, architecture, sculpture, painting, and terracotta figurines all over India.
  • This period is often referred to as the Golden Age of Ancient India.
  • Brick Temples: Bhitargaon in Kanpur, Bhitari in Ghazipur, and Deogarh in Jhansi.
  • The Buddhist University at Nalanda was set up in the 5th century.

Shams-ud-din Iltutmish (c. 1211-1236 AD)

  • Considered the real founder of the Delhi Sultanate.
  • Made Delhi the capital of the empire.
  • Suppressed revolts by ambitious nobles and Aibak's sons.
  • Led expeditions against Rajputs in Ranthambore, Jalor, and Mewar.
  • His governing class comprised two groups of foreign origin: Turkish slave officers and Taziks.
  • Introduced the silver coin (tanka) and the copper coin (jital).
  • Organised the Iqta system.
  • Introduced reforms in civil administration and the army (which was now paid and recruited).
  • Established an official nobility of slaves known as Turkan-i-Chalgani or Chalisa (a group of forty powerful Turkish nobles).
  • On February 18, 1229, the Khalifa from Baghdad sent a decree recognising the independent status of the Delhi Sultanate.
  • Known as the 'father of Tomb building' (built Sultan Garhi).
  • Completed the construction of Qutub Minar.
  • Saved the Delhi Sultanate from Chengiz Khan by refusing shelter to Khwarizm Shah, whom Chengiz Khan was pursuing.

Razia Sultan (c. 1236-1240 AD)

  • The first and last Muslim woman ruler of medieval India.
  • The first rebellion against her was led by Kabir Khan, Governor of Lahore.
  • Altunia, the Governor of Bhatinda, also revolted against her.
  • She was defeated and taken prisoner by Altunia, whom she subsequently married.
  • While moving towards Delhi with Altunia, she was defeated by Bahram Shah (Iltutmish's son).
  • Deserted by her soldiers, she was murdered by robbers.

Successors of Razia Sultan

  • Bahram Shah (AD 1240-1242)
  • Ala-ud-din Masud Shah (AD 1242-1246)
  • Nasir-ud-din Mahmud (AD 1246-1266) - He was the grandson of Iltutmish.

Ghiyas-ud-din Balban (c. 1266-1287 AD)

  • Ascended the throne in 1266-67 AD.
  • Ordered the separation of the military department (Diwan-i-Ariz) from the Finance Department (Diwan-i-Wizarat).
  • Emphasised the divine nature of kingship, stating it was the vice-regency of God on Earth (Niyabat-i-Khudai) and the king was the shadow of God (Zil-i-Ilahi).
  • Abandoned the Chalisa (Turkan-i-Chalgani).
  • Introduced the Persian court customs of Sijdah (prostration) and Paibos (kissing the monarch's feet).
  • Started the Nauroz festival.
  • Took strong measures to safeguard the North-West frontier against Mongol invasions.
  • Was deeply racist and excluded non-Turks from administration.
  • His son Mohammed's death was a major blow to him and his dynasty.
  • The last ruler of his dynasty was Kaiqubad, who was killed by Jalal-ud-din Khilji.

The Khiljis (c. 1290-1320 AD)

  • The ascendancy of the Khiljis is known as the 'Khilji Revolution'.
  • This marked the end of the monopolisation of power by the Turkish nobility and racial dictatorship.
  • The accession of Jalal-ud-din Firoz Khilji signified a 'revolution' in the political and cultural history of medieval India.

Jalal-ud-din Firoz Khilji (c. 1290-1296 AD)

  • Ascended the throne at the age of 70.
  • Married one of his daughters to the Mongol leader Ulugh Khan (a descendant of Chengiz Khan) to foster goodwill.
  • A significant event of his reign was the invasion of Devagiri (capital of the Yadava kingdom in the Deccan) by his nephew and son-in-law, Ala-ud-din Khilji (Governor of Kara).

Ala-ud-din Khilji (c. 1296-1316 AD)

  • His first major conquest was the rich kingdom of Gujarat, ruled by the Vaghela King Karna.
  • In 1299, his army, under Ulugh Khan and Nusrat Khan, invaded Anhilwad, the capital of Gujarat.
  • During the plunder of the port of Cambay, Nusrat Khan acquired a Hindu-turned-Muslim slave named Kafur (also known as Hazar Dinari).
  • Kafur later rose to become a great Military General and the Malik Naib of Ala-ud-din.
  • After Gujarat, he conquered Ranthambore in 1300-1301 from Hamir Deva.
  • In 1303 AD, he attacked Chittor, the capital of Mewar, ruled by Gahlot King Ratan Singh.
  • Queen Padmini committed Jauhar after her husband's defeat in Chittor.

Deccan Sultanates

    • Greatest ruler: Ibrahim Adil Shah, who introduced 'Dakhini' in place of Persian language.

  • Qutub Shahis of Golconda (1518-1687)

    • Built the famous Golconda fort and made it his capital.

    • Annexed by Adil Shahis of Bijapur.

Religious Movements

The Sufis

  • Sufism is a mystical Islamic belief and practice where Muslims seek divine love and knowledge through direct personal experience of God.
  • Sufis are against the orthodoxy and fanaticism of religion.
  • In India, Sufism appealed to both Muslims and Hindus, and Sufi saints became venerated figures for all.
  • **Ziyarat**: Visiting pilgrimage sites associated with Muhammad or venerated figures like Sufi saints.
  • **Khanqah**: The abode of Sufis.
  • During the 13th century, Sufism was divided into 14 silsilas (branches) in India.

The Chistis

  • Founder: Khwaza Abu-e-Chisti.
  • Popularised in India by Moin-ud-din Chisti. His tomb is in Ajmer, Rajasthan.
  • Main disciple of Moin-ud-din: Khwaza Qutub-ud-din Bakhtiyar Kaki (Qutub Minar was named after him).
  • Famous saint Nizam-ud-din Auliya saw the reign of seven Delhi Sultans. He was also known as Mahboob-i-Ilahi (beloved of God) and Sultan-ul-Auliya (king of the saints). His tomb is in Delhi.
  • Sheikh Nasir-ud-din (Chirag-i-Delhi) was a disciple of Mahboob-i-Illahi.
  • Amir Khusrau and Al-Biruni were also followers of Chisti.

Qadiri Silsila

  • Founder in India: Sheikh Mohi-ud-din Qadir Zilani.
  • Popularised by Shah Nizamat Ullah and Makhdum Zilani.
  • Dara Shikoh (son of Shah Jahan) was a disciple of Mullah Shah Badakshi.
  • Dara Shikoh wrote the biographies of saints: Safinat-ul-Auliya and Sakinat-ul-Auliya.
  • Dara Shikoh also translated books such as Sir-e-Akbar and Sir-e-Asrar.

Naqshabandi Silsila

  • Founder in India: Khwaza Baha-ud-din Naqshabandi.
  • Popularised by Khwaza Khwand Mahmud, whose centre was in Kashmir.
  • Other saints: Baqi-Billah, Shahwali ullah, Khwaza Mir Dard Naqshaband.
  • Mir Dard wrote Dard-e-Dil, Sham-e-Mahfil, and Ilm-ul-Khitab.

Other Famous Silsilah

  • Firdausi, Quadiri, Satari, etc.

Bhakti Movement

  • Bhakti is a devotional worship of God with the ultimate objective of attaining Moksha (Salvation).
  • Initially, the Bhakti Movement started in South India in the form of Alvars and Nayanars.
  • Gradually, the Bhakti Movement spread to North India, flourishing in the 13th century.
  • Bhakti saints were against casteism, ritualism, and religious sacrifices.
  • They professed equal participation of women in religious practices.
  • These saints used local dialects to promote their ideas and communicate with the masses.
  • The Bhakti Movement is divided into two branches: Nirguna and Saguna.

Nirguna Saints

  • **Guru Nanak**
    • Born at Talwandi in Lahore.
    • Propagated Monotheism, Hindu-Muslim unity, and denounced idol worship.
    • His disciple Mardana played Rabas.
    • He established the Sikh religion.
    • His teachings are found in Guru Granth Sahib.
  • **Dadu Dayal**
    • Born in Ahmedabad to Muslim parents, brought up by a Hindu.
    • His teachings are collected in a book called Bani.
    • His disciples were Sundaradasa, Rajjab, Bakham, and Warid.
    • He founded the Brahma sect or Param Brahma Sampradaya.
  • **Kabir**
    • Opposed to caste, creed, and idol worship.
    • Propagated Hindu-Muslim unity.
    • His works include Sabada Doha, Holi, Rekhtal, etc.
    • Verses of Kabir, Namdev, Ravidas, Dhanna, Pipa, etc., were included in Adi Granth.

The Marathas

Shivaji (1627-1680)

  • Creator of the Maratha nation, he united Maratha chiefs from Malwa, Konkan, and Desh regions to establish a kingdom.
  • Born in 1627 in the hill fort of Shivner.
  • Took control of his hereditary Jagir in 1647 after the death of his guardian Konadev.
  • Began his military career at a young age, capturing the fort of Toran in 1656.
  • From 1656, he started capturing many other forts from the local officers of Bijapur.
  • Raided Bijapur, leading Ali Adil Shah of Bijapur to send his General Afzal Khan to capture Shivaji. Shivaji killed Afzal Khan using a weapon called Baghnakh (tiger's claw).
  • Began attacking Mughal territories. Aurangzeb sent Shaista Khan, the Viceroy of the Deccan, against him. Shaista Khan captured Poona, but Shivaji outwitted the Mughals in 1663.
  • Aurangzeb then sent Prince Muazzam, and upon his failure, Mirza Raja Jai Singh of Amber was dispatched. Raja Jai Singh won victories and besieged Shivaji in Purandhar in 1665.
  • At Jai Singh's persuasion, Shivaji visited the Mughal Court of Agra but was detained. He escaped in 1666 and resumed his conquests.
  • In 1674, Shivaji made Raigarh his capital, celebrated his coronation, and assumed the title of 'Chhatrapati'.
  • Died in 1680 at the age of 53.

Shivaji's Successors

  • Sambhaji: Shivaji's son, ascended the throne despite a hostile faction supporting his step-brother Rajaram. His raiding of Mughal territories and sheltering of Akbar II (Aurangzeb's rebel son) led Aurangzeb to capture and execute him in 1689.
  • Rajaram: Crowned King after Sambhaji. He created the new post of Pratinidhi, increasing the total number of ministers to nine.
  • Tara Bai: Rajaram's widow, ascended the throne after his death.
  • Shahu: Sambhaji's son, released from Mughal captivity in 1707. He had to contend with his aunt Tara Bai for the Maratha throne. The period of Peshwa domination began during his reign.

Revenue Administration (Shivaji)

  • Shivaji abolished the Jagirdari system and replaced it with the Ryotwari system.
  • He brought about changes in the position of hereditary revenue officials like Deshmukhs, Deshpandes, Patils, and Kulkarnis.
  • Shivaji strictly supervised the Mirasdars (those with hereditary rights in land).
  • He considerably reduced the powers of these officials through close supervision and strict collection of revenue.
  • The appointment of revenue officials (Subahdars or Karkuns) in charge of revenue administration of Prants helped establish a strong revenue system.
  • The revenue system was patterned on the Kathi system of Malik Ambar, where land was carefully measured with a measuring rod (kathi).
  • Revenue assessment was made after a careful survey and classification of lands based on quality and yield.
  • The state's share was fixed at two-fifths of the gross produce.
  • Cultivators had the option of paying either in cash or kind.
  • A new revenue assessment was completed by Annaji Datto in AD 1679.

The Peshwas

  • The period of Peshwa domination in Maratha history began during Shahu's reign.
  • Balaji Vishwanath: Appointed Peshwa by King Shahu in 1713. He was an able administrator and an excellent diplomat.
  • Baji Rao I: Son of Balaji Vishwanath, served as Peshwa from 1720 to 1740. During his tenure, the Maratha kingdom was transformed into an empire.
  • Balaji Baji Rao: Succeeded Baji Rao I and was formally made the head of the state after King Shahu's death in 1749.
  • During Balaji Baji Rao's reign, the Maratha empire expanded further, and the Maratha Army overran Delhi.
  • The Marathas came into conflict with Ahmed Shah Abdali of Afghanistan, leading to the Third Battle of Panipat in 1761.
  • In the Third Battle of Panipat, the Maratha Army was completely routed, and the Peshwa's son, Vishwas Rao, and Sadashiva Rao Bhau were killed.
  • After the Third Battle of Panipat, four semi-independent Maratha states emerged: Baroda (ruled by Gaikwad), Nagpur (ruled by Bhonsle), Indore (ruled by Holkar), and Gwalior (ruled by Scindhia). The Peshwa continued to rule from Poona.
  • Baji Rao II: The last Peshwa. He signed the Treaty of Bassein in 1802, entering into a subsidiary alliance with the British, which led to the gradual diminution of the Maratha kingdom.

Land Revenue Systems

Permanent Settlement

  • Introduction: Introduced in Bengal, Bihar-Orissa, districts of Banaras, and Northern districts of Madras by Lord Cornwallis in 1793. Sir John Shore planned this settlement.
  • Key Features:
    • Declared zamindars as the owners of the land.
    • Zamindars kept 1/11th of the collected revenue, while the British received a fixed share of 10/11th.
    • Zamindars were free to fix the rent rate, often leading to exploitation of tenants.
    • Creation of multi-level ranks of collectors under the zamindar.

Ryotwari Settlement

  • Introduction: Introduced in Bombay, Madras, and Assam. Recommended by Munro and Charles Reed.
  • Key Features:
    • Direct settlement was made between the Government and the Ryots (cultivators).
    • Revenue was based on the quality of the soil and the nature of the crop.
    • Revenue was fixed for a period not exceeding 30 years.
    • Based on the Scientific Rent Theory of Ricardo.
    • Improved the security of the cultivator's position.

Mahalwari System

  • Introduction: Introduced in the areas of the Ganga valley, North-West Frontier Province (NWFP), parts of Central India, and Punjab.
  • Key Features:
    • Revenue settlement was made with the village or estates with landlords.
    • Settlement was made with the village, which maintained a form of common ownership known as 'Bhai Chara', or with 'Mahals' (groups of villages).
    • Revenue was periodically revised.

Early Gandhian Movements and Nationalist Upsurge

Champaran Satyagraha (1917)

  • Mahatma Gandhi established direct contact with ordinary people through tours in rural areas, discussing their concerns in their own language. This was a novel political technique, not previously practiced by educated Congress leaders.
  • For the first time, peasants were drawn into political agitation under a new type of leadership.
  • Gandhi's magnetic personality began to draw multitudes, earning him the titles of 'Mahatma' and the nickname 'Bapu'.
  • Under pressure from the Government of India, the Government of Bihar appointed a committee of enquiry in June 1917.
  • The recommendations of this committee were implemented by the Champaran Agrarian Act of 1917. Gandhi was also a member of this committee.
  • Leaders associated with Gandhiji in this Satyagraha included J.B. Kripalani, Rajendra Prasad, Mahadev Desai, and Narhari Parikh.
  • The book Neel Darpan, written by Dinbandhu Mitra, was based on this movement.

Ahmedabad Satyagraha (1918)

  • While Gandhi was in Bihar, Shrimati Anasuyabai informed him about the poor conditions of workers in Ahmedabad mills and requested his intervention.
  • The severe plague of 1917-18 led to a significant decline in the number of workers in Ahmedabad. To attract workers, mill owners initially paid 75% of wages as a 'plague bonus'.
  • The situation worsened when mill owners declared a lockout on February 22, 1918.
  • Mahatma Gandhi intervened in the dispute between workers and mill owners, undertaking a fast unto death to compel a compromise.
  • The issue was resolved when mill owners agreed to give 35% of wages as a bonus, which was accepted by the workers.

Kheda Satyagraha (1918)

  • This movement is described as the first Non-Cooperation Movement in India.
  • In 1917, most of the kharif crops of farmers in the Kheda district of Gujarat were destroyed due to heavy rains, making them unable to pay land revenue to the government.
  • When the government refused the peasants' demand to remit land revenues, Gandhiji advised them to withhold payment and launch a struggle against the government on March 22, 1918.
  • Gandhiji, along with lieutenants like Vallabhbhai Patel (who became his follower during this Satyagraha) and Indulal Yagnik, toured villages to encourage the peasants.

Home Rule Leagues (1916)

  • Inspired by the Irish rebellion, Mrs. Annie Besant (September 1916) and B.G. Tilak (April 1916) established the Home Rule Leagues.
  • B.G. Tilak linked 'Swaraj' with the demand for the formation of linguistic states and education in vernacular languages.
  • Tilak gave the popular slogan: "Freedom is my birth right and I shall have it."

Lucknow Pact (1916)

  • The Lucknow session of the Indian National Congress (INC) in 1916 was significant due to two major developments:
  • The re-admission of the extremists, who had been expelled from the INC nine years earlier.
  • The formation of an alliance between the Congress and the Muslim League.

Rowlatt Act (1919)

  • The government passed the Rowlatt Act in March 1919.
  • This act empowered the British Government to detain any person without trial.
  • It was regarded as a serious betrayal of the promises made by the government during the World War period.

Jallianwala Bagh Tragedy (1919)

  • People were agitating against the arrest of their popular leaders, Dr. Saif-ud-din Kitchlew and Dr. Satyapal.
  • On April 13, 1919, Baisakhi Day, hundreds of people were massacred and several thousand wounded in Jallianwala Bagh, where they had assembled for a protest meeting against the government's repressive policies.
  • Troops led by General Dyer opened fire on the unarmed men, women, young, old, Hindus, and Muslims.
  • This event was regarded as the worst massacre during the entire freedom struggle.
  • The Hunter Commission was appointed to inquire into the tragedy.
  • Sardar Udham Singh killed General Dyer on March 13, 1940, while the latter was addressing a meeting in Caxton Hall in London.

The Khilafat Movement (1920-22)

  • The All India Khilafat Conference was held in Delhi in November 1919.
  • Gandhiji was the head of the Khilafat Committee, and Maulana Abul Kalam Azad also led the movement.
  • A Khilafat Manifesto was later published, calling upon the British to protect the Khalifa (Caliphate).

Non-Cooperation Movement (1920-22)

  • The Non-Cooperation Movement was launched under the leadership of Mahatma Gandhi.
  • It aimed to press three main demands:
    1. The Khilafat issue.
    2. The redressal of the Punjab wrongs (referring to the Jallianwala Bagh tragedy).
    3. The attainment of Swaraj (self-rule).

Simla Conference (1945)

  • Date: June 25, 1945
  • Convened by: Lord Wavell
  • Purpose: To discuss proposals (Wavell Plan) for India's future.
  • Participants: Leaders of both the Indian National Congress and the Muslim League.
  • Issue: Differences arose between the parties on the issue of representation of the Muslim community.
  • Outcome: Resulted in a deadlock. Lord Wavell announced the failure of his efforts on July 14, 1945.

The Cabinet Mission Plan (1946)

  • Announcement: The Attlee Government announced in February 1946, the decision to send a high-powered mission to India.
  • Members: Three British Cabinet members – Pethick Lawrence, Stafford Cripps, and A.V. Alexander.
  • Purpose: To find ways and means for a negotiated peaceful transfer of power in India.
  • Key Proposal: The mission rejected the Muslim League's demand for Pakistan and proposed a Two-Tier Federal Plan.
  • Initial Acceptance: Initially accepted by both the Congress and the Muslim League.
  • Later Developments:
    • The Muslim League eventually decided to keep away from the plan.
    • The Muslim League joined the cabinet but decided to boycott the Constituent Assembly, which began its work of framing the Constitution in December 1946.
    • The Muslim League (June 6, 1946) and the Congress (June 24, 1946) accepted the long-term plan put forward by the Cabinet Mission.
    • Constituent Assembly Elections (July 1946): Elections were held in provincial assemblies for the Constituent Assembly. The Congress secured 209 out of the total 273 seats.
    • Muslim League's Withdrawal: On July 29, 1946, the League withdrew its acceptance of the long-term plan in reaction against Nehru's statement and gave a call for 'Direct Action' from August 16, 1946, to achieve Pakistan.

Interim Government (September 2, 1946)

  • Formation: Lord Wavell invited Jawaharlal Nehru, leader of the largest party, to form an Interim Government.
  • Sworn-in: September 2, 1946.
  • Composition: Composed of 12 members (including 3 Muslims) nominated by the Congress.
  • Vice-President: Jawaharlal Nehru.
  • Significance: It was the first time since the coming of the British that the Government of India was in Indian hands.
  • Muslim League's Stance: At first, the Muslim League refused to join. Later, it changed its stand and joined the Interim Government, but with the intention of not working sincerely.

Jinnah's Direct Action Resolution

  • Cause: The setback in the election to the Constituent Assembly forced the League to reject the Cabinet Mission Plan.
  • Call: Jinnah gave the call for 'Direct Action', postulating a campaign for the creation of Pakistan.
  • Withdrawal of Cabinet Plan Acceptance: Muslim League withdrew its acceptance of the Cabinet Plan on July 29, 1946.
  • Direct Action Day: From August 16, 1946, the country witnessed communal riots on an unprecedented scale. The League passed a Direct Action Resolution condemning both the British Government and Congress on August 16, 1946.
  • Pakistan Day: March 27, 1947, was celebrated as Pakistan Day by Jinnah.

Constituent Assembly

  • First Meeting: The Constituent Assembly met in New Delhi on December 9, 1946.
  • Participation: Met without the participation of the Muslim League.
  • President: Rajendra Prasad was elected as its President.

Mountbatten Plan (June 3, 1947)

  • Date: June 3, 1947.
  • Key Provisions:
    • Punjab and Bengal Legislative Assemblies would meet in two groups (Hindus and Muslims) to vote for partition.
    • In case of partition, two dominions and two Constituent Assemblies would be created.
    • Sindh would take its own decision.
    • A provision for a referendum was provided for NWFP (North-West Frontier Province) and Sylhet (a district of Bengal) to decide their fate.
    • Freedom would come on August 15, 1947.

The Partition of India

  • Boundary Commission: A Boundary Commission would be set up if partition was to be effected.
  • Indian Independence Act, 1947: In July 1947, the British Parliament ratified the Mountbatten Plan as the 'Independence of India' Act, 1947.
  • Implementation: The Act was implemented on August 15, 1947.
  • Pakistan's Independence: Pakistan became independent on August 14, 1947.
    • M.A. Jinnah became the first Governor-General of Pakistan.
  • India's Independence: At midnight of August 15, 1947, India became free.
    • Jawaharlal Nehru proclaimed it to be the nation with his famous 'Tryst with Destiny' speech.
    • On the morning of August 15, 1947, Lord Mountbatten was sworn-in as Governor-General.
    • Jawaharlal Nehru was sworn-in as the first Prime Minister of free India.
  • Princely States: At the time of freedom, there were 562 small and big princely states. Sardar Patel, the first Home Minister, played a crucial role in their integration.

Major Revolutions

Revolutions throughout human history vary widely in methods, duration, and motivating ideology, leading to major changes in culture, economy, and socio-political institutions.

The Renaissance

  • A period in European history from the 14th to 17th centuries, regarded as the cultural bridge between the Middle Ages and Modern History.
  • Its intellectual basis was humanism, derived from the rediscovery of classical Greek Philosophy, exemplified by Protagoras's saying, "Man is the measure of all things".
  • In politics, it contributed to the development of diplomacy customs and conventions.
  • In science, it led to an increased reliance on observation and inductive reasoning.
  • During this period, money and art were intertwined, with artists depending on patrons who, in turn, needed money to foster artistic talent.

Reformation

  • A schism from the Roman Catholic Church initiated by Martin Luther and continued by John Calvin and other early Protestant reformers in early 16th century Europe.
  • Luther began by criticising the selling of indulgences, insisting the Pope had no authority over Purgatory and that the Catholic doctrine of the merits of saints had no foundation in the Gospel.
  • The new movement decisively influenced the Church of England after 1547 under Edward VI and Elizabeth I.
  • The Church of England had been made independent under Henry VIII in the early 1530s for political rather than religious reasons.

Industrial Revolution

  • Started around the second half of the 18th century, witnessing changes in manufacturing processes from slow, expensive hand production to cheaper machine production.
  • Originated in England to fulfil growing domestic and international demands for goods.
  • Factors contributing to its start in England:
    • Great deposits of coal and iron ores.
    • A politically stable society and the world's leading colonial power, providing raw materials and markets.
    • Increased demand for goods led to the rise of mechanisation and the factory system.
    • A steadily growing agricultural sector during the 18th century allowed the British population to increase.
  • Started chiefly from the Textile Industry.
  • Key inventions:
    • John Kay (1733): Flying Shuttle (for faster weaving).
    • Edmund Cartwright (1785): Powerloom.
    • Elly Whitley: Cotton ginning machine (cotton gin).
    • James Watt: Steam engine.
  • The first railway line was built between Manchester and Liverpool in 1830.
  • By 1815, the growth of production increased Great Britain's wealth, making England the greatest banker and largest carrier of goods in the world.
  • By the close of the 19th century, science and technology became an integral part of Western society due to the Industrial Revolution.

Indian Music

  • Developed through complex interaction between different people of different races and cultures over thousands of years.
  • India's classical music tradition, including Carnatic and Hindustani music, has a history spanning millennia and developed over several eras.
  • Indian music is of two types:
    • Marga-Sangeet: Mystical
    • Desi Sangeet: Secular
  • Indian music is divided into ragas or melody types.

Ragas

  • A gamut of several notes woven into a composition.
  • Can be sung without any instrumental accompaniment, but generally take tabla (drum) and stringed instruments.
  • They are sung at particular seasons and times of the day or night.
  • Indian classical music consists of 6 principle ragas and 30 raginis.
  • Music is adapted to the season of the year, hours of the day, and mood of the performer.
  • The Indian year is divided into 6 seasons, and each season has its own raga.
  • The principle ragas are Bhairavi, Hindol, Megha, Sriraga, Deepak, and Malkaus.

Hindustani Classical Music

  • The North Indian style of Indian classical music found throughout the Northern Indian subcontinent.
  • Also called North Indian Classical Music or Shastriya Sangeet.
  • A tradition that originated in Vedic ritual chants and has been evolving since the 12th century CE.

Carnatic Music

  • A system of music commonly associated with the Southern part of the Indian sub-continent.
  • Area roughly confined to 4 modern States of India: Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Kerala, and Tamil Nadu.
  • The main emphasis is on vocal music; most compositions are written to be sung.
  • Even when played on instruments, they are meant to be performed in Gayaki (singing) style.
  • Carnatic music was mainly patronised by the local kings of the Kingdom of Mysore and Kingdom of Travancore in the 18th through 20th centuries.

Indian Dance Forms

  • Dancing is one of the most ancient arts in Indian culture.
  • Established its roots in the Indian soil as early as the Vedic times.
  • Deeply associated with religious rites, representing the supposed performances of the Gods and Goddesses themselves.
  • Maintained the divine and spiritual concepts of the race.
  • The religious purpose being diverse, the styles of dance were equally varied, including Classical dances, Folk dances, and Tribal dances in India.

Indian Constitution – Key Provisions and Amendments

Official Languages (Articles 343‑351)

  • Article 343: Declares Hindi in Devanagari script as the official language of the Union.
  • Article 348: Provides for the use of English in the Supreme Court, High Courts, and for the enactment of Acts, Bills, etc.
  • Emphasis on promotion of Hindi and mother‑tongues, especially in primary education.

Amendment Procedure (Article 368)

  • An amendment can be initiated only by the introduction of a Bill in either House of Parliament; State Legislatures cannot initiate amendments.
  • The Bill may be introduced by a Minister or a Private Member and does not require prior permission of the President.
  • Each House must pass the Bill separately; there is no provision for a joint sitting.
  • The President must give assent; he cannot withhold assent or return the Bill.

Valour Awards (Wartime Gallantry)

  • Param Vir Chakra (PVC)
    • India's highest military decoration for valour or self‑sacrifice in the presence of the enemy.
    • Instituted on 26 Jan 1950.
    • Design: Circular bronze disc (1.375 inches) with a purple ribbon; emblem of India at centre, vajra motif on reverse.
    • First awardee: Major Somnath Sharma.
  • Maha Vir Chakra (MVC)
    • Second highest wartime gallantry award.
    • Instituted on 26 Jan 1950.
    • Design: Embossed Hindi and English inscription with two lotus flowers; half‑white/half‑orange ribbon (≈3.2 cm wide).
    • First awardee: Lt. Col. Dewan Ranjit Rai.
  • Vir Chakra
    • Third in precedence among wartime gallantry awards.
    • Instituted on 26 Jan 1950.

Peace‑Time Gallantry Awards

  • Ashoka Chakra – Instituted 4 Jan 1952.
  • Kirti Chakra – Instituted 15 Aug 1947 (effective from 4 Jan 1952).
  • Shaurya Chakra – Instituted 15 Aug 1947 (effective from 4 Jan 1952).
  • All three may be awarded posthumously and recognise valour, courageous action, or self‑sacrifice away from the battlefield.

United Nations – Overview

  • Founded: 1945; 193 member states (as of latest data).
  • Principal Organs (6):
    1. General Assembly – HQ New York; each member state has one vote; meets annually.

    4. Trusteeship Council – Established 1945; suspended 1994 after the last trust territory (Palau) gained independence.

    6. Secretariat – headed by the Secretary‑General (current: António Guterres); assists the UN organs.
