  <h2 style="color:var(--accent);margin-bottom:16px;border-bottom:1px solid var(--border);padding-bottom:8px;font-weight:600;">Buddhism and Jainism</h2>

  <h3>1. Historical Context and Origins</h3>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Chronological placement</strong>: Both traditions arose in the 6th–5th centuries BCE in the eastern Gangetic plains, contemporaneous with the later Vedic period and the rise of the <strong>Mahajanapadas</strong>.</li>
    <li><strong>Socio‑political milieu</strong>: Widespread disillusionment with the ritualistic Brahmanical order, rapid urbanisation, and the emergence of the <strong>Śramaṇa movement</strong> that questioned priestly authority.</li>
    <li><strong>Foundational personalities</strong>:
      <ul>
        <li>Siddhartha Gautama (c. 563–483 BCE) – later known as the <strong>Buddha</strong>.</li>
        <li>Vardhamana (Mahavira) (c. 540–468 BCE) – 24th <strong>Tirthankara</strong> of Jainism.</li>
      </ul>
    </li>
    <li><strong>Geographical heartland</strong>: Kapilavastu (present‑day Nepal) for Buddhism; the region of Vardhamana in present‑day Bihar for Jainism.</li>
  </ul>

  <h3>2. Core Doctrines and Philosophical Tenets</h3>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Rejection of Vedic authority</strong>: Both traditions deny the infallibility of the Vedas and the supremacy of the Brahmin priesthood.</li>
    <li><strong>Concept of liberation</strong>:
      <ul>
        <li>Jainism – attainment of <strong>kevala jñāna</strong> (omniscience) leading to liberation from the cycle of birth‑death.</li>
        <li>Buddhism – cessation of suffering through the realisation of <strong>Nirvāṇa</strong>, the extinction of craving.</li>
      </ul>
    </li>
    <li><strong>Ethical framework</strong>:
      <ul>
        <li>Jainism – <strong>Five Mahavratas</strong>: Ahimsa, Satya, Asteya, Brahmacharya, Aparigraha.</li>
        <li>Buddhism – <strong>Noble Eightfold Path</strong>: Right View, Intention, Speech, Action, Livelihood, Effort, Mindfulness, Concentration.</li>
      </ul>
    </li>
    <li><strong>Samsāra and Karma</strong>: Both accept rebirth but differ in mechanism – Jainism stresses the physical influx of karmic particles bound to the soul, whereas Buddhism treats karma as volitional mental formations.</li>
    <li><strong>Metaphysical stance</strong>:
      <ul>
        <li>Jainism – dualistic: an eternal soul (<em>jīva</em>) distinct from non‑souls (<em>ajiva</em>).</li>
        <li>Buddhism – non‑essentialist: denial of an eternal self (<em>anatta</em>).</li>
      </ul>
    </li>
  </ul>

  <h3>3. Canonical Literature</h3>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Jain texts</strong>:
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Agamas</strong> – originally 45 scriptures (later reduced to 24) attributed to Mahavira’s disciples.</li>
        <li><strong>Kalpa Sutras</strong> – biographies of the Tirthankaras and monastic codes.</li>
      </ul>
    </li>
    <li><strong>Buddhist scriptures</strong>:
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Tripiṭaka</strong> (Three Baskets):
          <ul>
            <li><em>Sutras</em> – discourses of the Buddha.</li>
            <li><em>Vinaya</em> – monastic discipline.</li>
            <li><em>Abhidhamma</em> – philosophical analysis.</li>
          </ul>
        </li>
        <li>Early Buddhist Councils – traditionally three (First: c. 483 BCE, Second: c. 383 BCE, Third: c. 250 BCE) that consolidated the canon.</li>
      </ul>
    </li>
  </ul>

  <h3>4. Monastic Orders and Organizational Structure</h3>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Jain monasticism</strong>:
      <ul>
        <li>Two major sects – <strong>Śvētāmbara</strong> (white‑clad) and <strong>Digambara</strong> (sky‑clad).</li>
        <li>Hierarchy – <em>Acharya</em> (head), <em>Upadhyaya</em> (teacher), <em>Priyavrata</em> (senior monk).</li>
      </ul>
    </li>
    <li><strong>Buddhist monasticism</strong>:
      <ul>
        <li>Major schools – <strong>Theravāda</strong> (Southern), <strong>Mahāyāna</strong> (Northern), and later <strong>Vajrayāna</strong> (Tibetan).</li>
        <li>Administrative body – the <em>Sangha</em>, governed by the <em>Vinaya</em> code.</li>
      </ul>
    </li>
  </ul>

  <h3>5. Patronage and State Support</h3>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Emperor Ashoka (r. 268–232 BCE)</strong>:

    </li>
    <li><strong>Jain royal patronage</strong>:
      <ul>

        <li>Later dynasties – notably the <strong>Gupta</strong> and <strong>Rashtrakuta</strong> – funded Jain temple construction, especially in western India.</li>
      </ul>
    </li>
  </ul>

      </ul>
    </li>

  <h3>7. Sectarian Developments and Philosophical Schools</h3>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Jain sects</strong>:
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Śvētāmbara</strong> – accept the possibility of women attaining liberation; preserve a more extensive canonical literature.</li>
        <li><strong>Digambara</strong> – emphasize radical asceticism; reject the validity of female ordination.</li>
      </ul>
    </li>
    <li><strong>Buddhist schools</strong>:
      <ul>

        <li><strong>Mahāyāna</strong> – introduces the <em>Bodhisattva</em> ideal; scriptural corpus includes the <strong>Lotus Sutra</strong> and the <strong>Heart Sutra</strong>.</li>
        <li><strong>Vajrayāna</strong> – “Diamond Vehicle”; incorporates tantric practices; flourished in Tibet and the Himalayan region.</li>
      </ul>
    </li>
  </ul>

  <h3>8. Socio‑Cultural Impact</h3>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Ethical reforms</strong>: Promotion of non‑violence (<em>ahimsa</em>) led to widespread vegetarianism among merchant classes and the establishment of animal‑shelter institutions (e.g., Ashokan animal hospitals).</li>
    <li><strong>Art and architecture</strong>:
      <ul>
        <li>Stupas (e.g., Sanchi, Amaravati) and rock‑cut chaityas as early Buddhist architectural forms.</li>
        <li>Jain temples exemplify intricate marble work; iconic examples include the <strong>Dilwara Temples</strong> and the <strong>Shravanabelagola</strong> monolith.</li>
        <li>Iconography – Buddha statues with the <em>abhaya mudra</em> and Jain Tirthankara idols in seated meditational posture.</li>
      </ul>
    </li>
    <li><strong>Literary contributions</strong>: Canonical texts inspired later works such as the <strong>Jataka tales</strong> (Buddhist moral stories) and the Jain‑circulated <strong>Pañcatantra</strong> (though primarily a Hindu work).</li>
    <li><strong>Influence on polity</strong>: Ashoka’s Dhamma policy introduced concepts of religious tolerance and welfare (planting shade trees, establishing wells) that prefigured later state‑craft in Indian history.</li>
  </ul>
