📖 Complete Theory

1. Overview of the Human Respiratory System

The system is divided into conducting zones (nasal cavity, pharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi) and respiratory zones (bronchioles, alveolar ducts, alveoli). The primary function is to bring oxygen (O₂) into the blood and expel carbon dioxide (CO₂). Protective mechanisms include nasal hairs, mucociliary clearance, sneezing, and the cough reflex – all essential for a soldier in dusty or chemical‑hazard environments.

2. Mechanics of Breathing

Breathing is a pressure‑driven process. During inspiration, the diaphragm contracts and moves caudally, while external intercostal muscles lift the ribs. This expands the thoracic cavity, dropping intra‑alveolar pressure below atmospheric (≈ –5 cm H₂O). Air rushes in. Expiration is usually passive; elastic recoil of lungs and chest wall pushes air out. During forced expiration (e.g., sprinting, combat), abdominal muscles contract, raising intra‑abdominal pressure.

3. Alveolar Gas Exchange

Alveoli are thin‑walled sacs surrounded by a dense capillary network. Gas exchange obeys Fick’s law:

V = (D × A × ΔP) / T

where V is the diffusion rate, D the diffusion coefficient, A the surface area, ΔP the partial pressure gradient, and T the thickness of the alveolar‑capillary membrane. Surfactant (dipalmitoyl‑phosphatidylcholine) lowers surface tension, keeping alveoli open and maximizing A.

4. Transport of Gases in Blood

O₂ binds to haemoglobin (Hb) forming oxy‑Hb; the relationship is depicted by the O₂‑Hb dissociation curve. Factors that shift the curve right (decreasing affinity) include ↑ 2,3‑DPG, ↓ pH (Bohr effect), ↑ temperature, and ↑ CO₂. At high altitudes, the right shift facilitates O₂ release to tissues.

5. High‑Altitude Physiology

At 3,000 m, barometric pressure drops from 760 mmHg to ≈ 523 mmHg, reducing PO₂ from 159 mmHg to ≈ 110 mmHg. The body reacts in three phases:

  • Acute phase (minutes‑hours): Hyperventilation driven by peripheral chemoreceptors → ↑ alveolar PO₂, ↓ PaCO₂ (respiratory alkalosis).
  • Acclimatization (days‑weeks): Renal compensation (excreting HCO₃⁻), ↑ erythropoietin → ↑ RBC mass, ↑ 2,3‑DPG.
  • Chronic adaptation (months‑years): Increased capillary density, mitochondrial efficiency, and altered ventilation patterns.

Failure to adapt leads to Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS), High‑Altitude Pulmonary Edema (HAPE), or High‑Altitude Cerebral Edema (HACE). Military personnel operating above 3,000 m must undergo staged acclimatization to avoid these life‑threatening conditions.

6. Clinical Correlates for Defence Personnel

Understanding respiratory physiology aids in:

  • Assessing fitness for high‑altitude deployment.
  • Managing smoke inhalation and chemical warfare exposure.
  • Optimizing breathing techniques for parachuting, scuba diving, and high‑G flight.
  • Diagnosing and treating hypoxia‑related performance decline.

7. Quick Calculations (Fick’s Law)

Example: If alveolar surface area is 70 m², diffusion coefficient for O₂ is 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ cm² s⁻¹ mmHg⁻¹, membrane thickness 0.5 µm, and ΔP = 40 mmHg, the O₂ diffusion rate is ≈ 560 mL min⁻¹ – sufficient for resting metabolism (≈ 250 mL min⁻¹).

🧠 Memory Tricks & Mnemonics

  • “ALVEOLI = A LARGE VENTILATION EQUIPMENT – O2 IN, CO2 OUT” – Reminds that alveoli have huge surface area for gas swap.
  • “HIGH‑ALTITUDE = HYPER‑VENTILATE – INCREASE G‑FORCE (G‑FORCE = “GO!”)”. Think of pilots shouting “GO!” before a steep climb.
  • “2,3‑DPG = 2‑Three‑D‑Guns = Right‑Shift Troops”. The “guns” (DPG) push O₂ release to troops.
  • “SURFactant = SURVIVOR FACTOR”. Without surfactant lungs collapse – just like a parachute without a pack.
  • “BOHR = BATTLE OF HYPER‑RESPIRATION”. Low pH (acid) fights O₂ binding, just as enemies fight each other.
  • “EPO = ENLISTED PILOT OXYGEN”. Soldiers at altitude need extra ‘pilot’ oxygen via RBCs.

⭐ Important Facts (Defence Exam Favourites)

  1. Alveolar surface area ≈ 70 m² – roughly the size of a tennis court.
  2. Average adult lung capacity ≈ 6 L; tidal volume ≈ 0.5 L.
  3. Partial pressure of O₂ at sea level ≈ 159 mmHg; at 5,500 m it falls to ≈ 70 mmHg.
  4. Surfactant is primarily dipalmitoyl‑phosphatidylcholine (DPPC).
  5. Fick’s law constant for O₂ diffusion in lung ≈ 1 × 10⁻⁵ cm² s⁻¹ mmHg⁻¹.
  6. Hyperventilation at 4,500 m can raise alveolar PO₂ by up to 20 mmHg.
  7. Acclimatization increases haemoglobin by 1–2 g/dL per week.
  8. 2,3‑DPG concentration rises from 4 mmol/L (sea level) to 6‑8 mmol/L at altitude.
  9. AMS incidence is ≈ 25 % at 2,500 m, 50 % at 4,500 m.
  10. HAPE mortality > 30 % if untreated; rapid descent is the cure.
  11. PaCO₂ falls to ≈ 30 mmHg during acute altitude exposure.
  12. Respiratory alkalosis is compensated by renal excretion of HCO₃⁻.
  13. O₂‑Hb dissociation curve shifts right with ↑ temperature, ↓ pH, ↑ CO₂, ↑ 2,3‑DPG.
  14. Diaphragmatic breathing improves VO₂max – used in army fitness drills.
  15. Carbon monoxide binds Hb with 250‑times affinity of O₂ – crucial for smoke inhalation training.
  16. Ventilation‑Perfusion (V/Q) mismatch is the most common cause of hypoxemia.
  17. High‑altitude training improves erythropoietin production – adopted by Indian Army for mountain units.
  18. The “H” in HAPE stands for “Hypoxic” – pulmonary vasoconstriction causes capillary leak.
  19. Maximum voluntary ventilation (MVV) ≈ 150 L/min for a healthy adult male.
  20. Chest wall compliance decreases with age – relevant for older officers.

🔢 Formula / Key Data Box

Fick’s Law: V = (D × A × ΔP) / T

Alveolar Gas Equation: PAO₂ = (FiO₂ × (PB – PH₂O)) – (PaCO₂ / R)

Where FiO₂ = 0.21, PH₂O = 47 mmHg, R = 0.8.

Ventilation (VE) = Tidal Volume (VT) × Respiratory Rate (f)

Normal VT ≈ 0.5 L, f ≈ 12 breaths/min → VE ≈ 6 L/min.

Altitude PO₂ Approximation: PO₂ = 0.21 × (PB – 47)

PB at 4,000 m ≈ 460 mmHg → PO₂ ≈ 87 mmHg.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • Confusing alveolar PO₂ with arterial PO₂ – remember the A‑a gradient (~5‑10 mmHg).
  • Ignoring the role of 2,3‑DPG in high‑altitude adaptation – it is a major right‑shift factor.
  • Using sea‑level barometric pressure for altitude calculations – always adjust PB.
  • Assuming hyperventilation always raises PaCO₂ – at altitude it actually lowers PaCO₂.
  • Mixing up the functions of surfactant and mucus – surfactant reduces surface tension; mucus traps particles.
  • Treating HAPE as a cardiac problem – it is purely hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction driven.
  • Over‑estimating the protective effect of CO₂ – high CO₂ actually worsens hypoxia via the Bohr effect.
  • Neglecting renal compensation in chronic altitude exposure – leads to persistent alkalosis.
