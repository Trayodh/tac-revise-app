📖 Complete Theory

The essence of heredity lies in the molecule that stores biological information – deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA). Discovered by Watson and Crick in 1953, DNA is a long polymer composed of nucleotides. Each nucleotide consists of a phosphate group, a deoxyribose sugar and one of four nitrogenous bases – adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G) or cytosine (C). The bases pair specifically (A‑T, G‑C) via hydrogen bonds, giving rise to the iconic double‑helix structure. The two strands run in opposite directions (antiparallel), a feature crucial for replication and transcription.

DNA Replication – The Blueprint Copy

Replication is a semi‑conservative process – each daughter DNA contains one original (parental) strand and one newly synthesised strand. It begins at specific origins of replication where the enzyme DNA helicase unwinds the double helix, forming replication forks. The leading strand is synthesised continuously in the 5’→3’ direction by DNA polymerase III. The lagging strand is synthesised discontinuously as Okazaki fragments, later joined by DNA ligase. Proofreading activity of DNA polymerase ensures high fidelity, limiting mutation rates to ~10⁻⁹ per base per replication.

Transcription – From DNA to RNA

Transcription converts the genetic code into a portable messenger – mRNA. It occurs in the nucleus (or nucleoid in prokaryotes). The RNA polymerase binds to the promoter region, unwinds the DNA locally, and synthesises a complementary RNA strand (A↔U, G↔C). The process terminates at a terminator sequence, releasing a pre‑mRNA that undergoes capping, poly‑A tail addition and splicing (removal of introns) before entering the cytoplasm.

Translation – Building Proteins

Translation is the decoding of mRNA into a polypeptide chain on ribosomes. Each three‑base codon on mRNA specifies an amino acid, brought by a matching tRNA carrying an anticodon. The ribosome has three sites – A (aminoacyl), P (peptidyl) and E (exit). Peptide bonds form as the ribosome moves along the mRNA, and translation ends at a stop codon (UAA, UAG, UGA). The resulting polypeptide folds into a functional protein, often assisted by chaperones.

Mendelian Genetics – Foundations of Inheritance

Gregor Mendel’s pea‑plant experiments gave us two fundamental laws:

  • Law of Segregation: Paired alleles separate during gamete formation, so each gamete receives only one allele per gene.
  • Law of Independent Assortment: Genes located on different chromosomes assort independently, producing varied genotype combinations.

A monohybrid cross (single trait) typically yields a 3:1 phenotypic ratio in the F₂ generation. A dihybrid cross (two traits) gives a 9:3:3:1 ratio, illustrating independent assortment. However, when genes are linked on the same chromosome, recombination frequency (< 50%) alters these ratios, allowing us to construct genetic maps measured in centimorgans (cM).

Types of Inheritance

Beyond classic Mendelian patterns, several special cases are vital for defence exams:

  • Sex‑linked inheritance: Genes on X chromosome; males (XY) express recessive traits directly (e.g., hemophilia).
  • Incomplete dominance: Heterozygote shows intermediate phenotype (e.g., red × white snapdragon → pink).
  • Codominance: Both alleles expressed equally (e.g., human ABO blood group – IA and IB).
  • Polygenic inheritance: Traits controlled by many genes (e.g., skin colour, height).
  • Multiple alleles: More than two alleles exist in the population (e.g., ABO blood groups).

Genetic Linkage & Mapping

When two genes lie close together on the same chromosome, they tend to be inherited together. The recombination frequency (RF) between them is calculated as:

RF (%) = (Number of recombinant offspring / Total offspring) × 100

A RF of 1% equals 1 centimorgan (cM). Mapping helps us locate disease genes, a skill useful in forensic DNA analysis and in assessing genetic disorders that could affect soldier fitness.

Application in Defence Context

Understanding genetics aids in:

  • Assessing hereditary disease risk in recruits (e.g., sickle‑cell trait confers malaria resistance – a double‑edged sword).
  • Forensic identification using DNA fingerprinting (STR profiling) for crime scene investigation and battlefield casualty identification.
  • Designing bio‑engineered crops with higher yield – essential for army logistics in remote areas.
  • Developing vaccines and understanding pathogen mutation rates (e.g., viral genome evolution).

Key Take‑aways

  • DNA is the universal genetic material; its replication ensures faithful transmission of information.
  • Transcription and translation are the two-step flow of genetic information (DNA → RNA → Protein).
  • Mendel’s laws explain segregation and assortment; deviations (linkage, incomplete dominance) enrich the genetic landscape.
  • Genetic calculations (Chi‑square, recombination frequency) are frequent in exam questions.
  • Real‑world relevance: forensic DNA, disease resistance, biotechnological applications.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • Confusing DNA replication (semi‑conservative) with transcription – remember replication copies DNA, transcription makes RNA.
  • Mixing up codominance with incomplete dominance – codominance shows both phenotypes; incomplete gives a blend.
  • Assuming all linked genes show 50% recombination – linkage reduces recombination below 50%.
  • Neglecting the role of the promoter in transcription – it’s the “launch pad” for RNA polymerase.
  • Using the wrong base pairing for RNA (T instead of U) – a frequent slip in MCQs.
  • Over‑looking sex‑linked inheritance in males – they have only one X chromosome.
  • Applying Mendelian ratios to polygenic traits – polygenic traits give a continuous distribution, not discrete ratios.
  • Forgetting to convert recombination frequency to cM before mapping – 1 % = 1 cM.
