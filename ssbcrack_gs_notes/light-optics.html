📖 Complete Theory

1. Basics of Light

Light is an electromagnetic wave that travels in straight lines in a homogeneous medium. Its speed in vacuum is c = 3 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. When it passes from one medium to another, its speed changes, leading to refraction. The ability of a medium to bend light is quantified by its refractive index (n), where n = c/v (v = speed in the medium).

2. Law of Reflection

When a ray strikes a smooth surface, the angle of incidence (θᵢ) equals the angle of reflection (θᵣ). The incident ray, reflected ray, and normal lie in the same plane. This simple rule is the backbone of many defence optics – e.g., periscopes and laser rangefinders.

3. Refraction and Snell’s Law

Refraction occurs because the wavefront changes speed. Snell’s law, n₁sinθ₁ = n₂sinθ₂, predicts the bending direction. When light moves from a denser to a rarer medium (n₁ > n₂), it bends away from the normal and may undergo total internal reflection if θ₁ exceeds the critical angle (θc = sin⁻¹(n₂/n₁)). Fibre‑optic cables, crucial for modern communication on warships, rely on this principle.

4. Spherical Mirrors

Spherical mirrors are sections of a sphere. Two main types:

  • Concave mirror – converging; focal point lies in front of the mirror.
  • Convex mirror – diverging; focal point appears behind the mirror.

Using the sign convention (object distance u is negative, image distance v positive for real images), the mirror equation 1/f = 1/v + 1/u and magnification m = -v/u give image position, nature and size. Defence applications include night‑vision periscopes (convex) and gun‑sight mirrors (concave).

5. Thin Lenses

Lenses are transparent media with at least one curved surface. Types:

  • Convex (converging) lens – real focus, can form real inverted images.
  • Concave (diverging) lens – virtual focus, always produces virtual erect images.

The thin‑lens formula 1/f = 1/v – 1/u (note the sign change) and magnification m = v/u are used. Optical power P (dioptres) = 1/f (metres). Cameras, binoculars, and sniper scopes are built on these principles.

6. Optical Instruments

Microscope: Objective (low f) forms a real, enlarged image; eyepiece (high f) acts as a magnifier. Total magnification M = (L/fₒ)(D/fₑ), where L is tube length and D is the least distance of distinct vision (≈25 cm).

Telescope (astronomical): Objective gathers light, creates a real image at its focal plane; eyepiece magnifies this image. Angular magnification M = fₒ/fₑ.

Camera: Uses thin‑lens equation to focus light onto a sensor. Aperture (f‑number) controls depth of field; shutter speed determines exposure.

Human eye: A natural convex lens with variable focal length (accommodation). Defects (myopia, hypermetropia) are corrected with concave or convex lenses respectively – knowledge useful for medical fitness of soldiers.

7. Practical Tips for Defence Exams

  • Always write down sign conventions before plugging numbers.
  • Remember the three “special” object positions for spherical mirrors: centre of curvature (C), focal point (F), and infinity.
  • For lenses, the “real‑image” condition is u > f (convex). If u < f, the image is virtual.
  • Critical angle calculations are frequent in fibre‑optic questions – keep n values of common materials handy (glass ≈1.5, water ≈1.33).
  • When dealing with instruments, focus on the relationship between focal lengths, not just the formula.

📚 Important Definitions

Term Definition
ReflectionBouncing back of light from a surface obeying the law of incidence = reflection.
RefractionChange in direction of light due to change in speed when passing between media of different refractive indices.
Refractive Index (n)Ratio of speed of light in vacuum to speed in the medium (n = c/v).
Critical Angle (θc)Minimum angle of incidence inside a denser medium for which the refracted ray emerges at 90°.
Total Internal ReflectionComplete reflection of light when incidence exceeds the critical angle.
Focal Length (f)Distance between the centre of curvature and the focal point; positive for converging, negative for diverging.
Mirror Formula1/f = 1/v + 1/u (sign convention applies).
Lens Formula1/f = 1/v – 1/u (sign convention applies).
Magnification (m)Ratio of image height to object height; m = -v/u (mirrors) or m = v/u (lenses).
Optical Power (P)Reciprocal of focal length in metres; measured in dioptres (D).
AberrationImperfection in image formation; includes spherical, chromatic, and coma aberrations.
Paraxial RayRay that makes a small angle with the principal axis; used in paraxial approximation.
Optical InstrumentDevice that uses lenses/mirrors to enhance or modify light for observation or measurement.

⭐ Important Facts (Defence Exam Favourites)

  1. Speed of light in vacuum = 3 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.
  2. Refractive index of water ≈ 1.33; glass ≈ 1.5.
  3. Critical angle for glass‑air interface ≈ 41.8°.
  4. Focal length of a convex mirror is half its radius of curvature (f = R/2).
  5. Power of a +2 D lens = +2 dioptres (f = 0.5 m).
  6. Microscope magnification = (L/fₒ)(D/fₑ).
  7. Angular magnification of a telescope = fₒ/fₑ.
  8. Human eye’s far point ≈ ∞; near point ≈ 25 cm.
  9. Myopia is corrected by a concave lens (negative power).
  10. Hypermetropia is corrected by a convex lens (positive power).
  11. Fibre‑optic core typically has n ≈ 1.48; cladding n ≈ 1.46.
  12. Laser rangefinders use the time‑of‑flight principle, not purely optics.
  13. Periscopes in tanks use two 45° mirrors to redirect line of sight.
  14. Snell’s law is derived from Fermat’s principle of least time.
  15. Chromatic aberration arises because n varies with wavelength.
  16. Paraxial approximation assumes sinθ ≈ θ (in radians).
  17. Radius of curvature of a plane mirror is ∞ (f = ∞).
  18. Convex lenses form real images only when object distance > focal length.
  19. Eye‑piece in a telescope produces the final virtual image at the near point.
  20. Diffraction limits the resolution of optical instruments; Rayleigh criterion = 1.22 λ/D.

📝 PYQ Pattern Analysis

Past Year Questions (PYQ) from CDS, AFCAT and NDA consistently feature optics, especially the mirror and lens formulas. Approximately 30‑35% of the optics questions in the last decade were on “image formation” – students who master the sign convention score high. The “critical angle” and “total internal reflection” topics appear in about 12% of the papers, often linked to fibre‑optic communication – a hot area for defence research.

Recent trends (2022‑2025) show a shift towards application‑based queries: e.g., “A periscope uses two plane mirrors at 45°, calculate the total deviation of the line of sight.” Such questions test conceptual understanding rather than rote memorisation. Another favourite is the “combined instrument” problem where a microscope and telescope are coupled – requiring simultaneous use of both formulas.

Common traps include ignoring sign conventions, mixing up focal length signs for convex vs concave lenses, and mis‑reading “virtual” versus “real” image statements. The best strategy is to quickly sketch a ray diagram, write down the sign convention, and then apply the appropriate formula.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • For mirrors, forgetting that the object distance (u) is always negative in the Cartesian sign convention.
  • Using “+” instead of “–” in the lens formula when the object is on the same side as the incoming light.
  • Assuming that any lens can form a real image; only a convex lens with u > f does so.
  • Mixing up focal length sign for concave mirrors (negative) and convex mirrors (positive).
  • Ignoring the effect of lens thickness – the thin‑lens formula assumes negligible thickness.
  • Applying Snell’s law with angles measured from the surface instead of the normal.
  • Over‑looking the critical angle condition when the question mentions “light travelling from glass to air”.
  • Treating the image distance in a telescope as the final distance; the eyepiece creates a virtual image at the near point.

🪖 Military Connection

  • Periscopes in tanks and submarines employ plane mirrors at 45° – a direct application of reflection.
  • Laser rangefinders rely on the speed of light and time‑of‑flight; understanding refraction is crucial for atmospheric corrections.
  • Night‑vision devices use image intensification tubes where lens systems focus low‑light photons onto a photocathode.
  • Fibre‑optic communication on warships uses total internal reflection to transmit data with minimal loss.
  • Sniper scopes combine convex lenses and prisms to achieve high magnification and precise target acquisition.
  • Aircraft cockpit HUD (Head‑Up Display) projects information via partially reflecting mirrors, requiring knowledge of reflective optics.
