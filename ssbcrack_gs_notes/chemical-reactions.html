📖 Complete Theory

A chemical reaction is a process in which reactants are transformed into products. The transformation obeys the law of conservation of mass and proceeds via breaking old bonds and forming new ones. Understanding reactions is crucial for defence chemistry because it underpins propellant chemistry, explosives, corrosion control, and life‑support systems.

1. Classification of Reactions

Reactions are grouped on the basis of the observable change in the reactants:

  • Synthesis (Combination): A + B → AB. Example: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O (combustion of hydrogen).
  • Decomposition: AB → A + B. Example: 2HgO → 2Hg + O₂ (thermal decomposition of mercury(II) oxide).
  • Single‑Replacement (Displacement): A + BC → AC + B. Example: Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂.
  • Double‑Replacement (Metathesis): AB + CD → AD + CB. Example: AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl↓ + NaNO₃.
  • Combustion: Hydrocarbon + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O (exothermic, releases large energy).

2. Rate of Reaction

The rate quantifies how fast reactants are consumed or products formed. It is expressed as:

Rate = - (1/ν) Δ[Reactant]/Δt = (1/ν) Δ[Product]/Δt

where ν is the stoichiometric coefficient. The rate law links rate to concentration of reactants:

Rate = k [A]^m [B]^n …

The exponents (m, n…) are determined experimentally and may differ from stoichiometric coefficients. k is the rate constant, temperature‑dependent as described by the Arrhenius equation:

k = A e^(-E_a/RT)

Key factors affecting rate:

  • Concentration – more molecules → more collisions.
  • Temperature – increases kinetic energy → higher fraction of molecules exceed activation energy (E_a).
  • Surface area – powdered solids expose more area.
  • Catalysts – provide an alternative pathway with lower E_a.
  • Nature of reactants – ionic reactions are generally faster than covalent ones.

3. Collision Theory & Activation Energy

Collision theory states that for a reaction to occur, reactant particles must collide with proper orientation and energy ≥ E_a. The fraction of collisions that are effective is given by the steric factor (p). The overall rate constant can be written as:

k = p Z e^(-E_a/RT)

where Z is the collision frequency. This explains why a catalyst (which raises p) accelerates the reaction without altering the thermodynamics.

4. Chemical Equilibrium

When the forward and reverse reactions occur at equal rates, the system reaches dynamic equilibrium. Concentrations of reactants and products become constant, though microscopic reactions continue. The equilibrium constant (K_eq) is a ratio of product activities to reactant activities, each raised to the power of their coefficients:

K_eq = ([C]^c [D]^d) / ([A]^a [B]^b)

A large K_eq (>1) indicates product‑favoured equilibrium; a small K_eq (<1) indicates reactant‑favoured.

5. Le Chatelier’s Principle

If a stress is applied to a system at equilibrium, the system shifts to counteract the stress:

  • Concentration change – add/remove reactant or product.
  • Pressure change – for gaseous equilibria, shift toward side with fewer moles when pressure ↑.
  • Temperature change – endothermic direction is favoured by heating, exothermic by cooling.
  • Catalyst – speeds both forward and reverse equally; no shift.

6. Applications in Defence

- Propellants (e.g., nitrocellulose) rely on rapid decomposition and gas evolution.

- Explosives such as RDX undergo fast exothermic decomposition, where controlling rate is vital for safety.

- Corrosion inhibition uses equilibrium concepts to maintain metal surface passivity.

⭐ Important Facts (Defence Exam Favourites)

  1. Combustion of a hydrocarbon is always exothermic.
  2. RDX (Cyclotrimethylenetrinitramine) decomposes explosively releasing N₂ gas.
  3. In a gaseous equilibrium, increasing pressure shifts the reaction toward the side with fewer moles.
  4. A catalyst does not alter the value of K_eq.
  5. Activation energy is the energy barrier between reactants and the transition state.
  6. Rate constant doubles for every 10 °C rise in temperature (approximation).
  7. Le Chatelier’s principle can be used to predict the effect of temperature on equilibrium.
  8. In the Haber process, iron acts as a catalyst for N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃.
  9. Decomposition reactions are generally endothermic.
  10. For a first‑order reaction, half‑life (t½) = 0.693/k.
  11. In a double‑replacement reaction, a precipitate, gas, or weak electrolyte indicates completion.
  12. Metal‑oxide reduction is vital for making propellant powders.
  13. Collision frequency (Z) depends on concentration and temperature.
  14. Stirring a solution increases the rate by dispersing reactant molecules.
  15. In the nitration of benzene, H₂SO₄ acts as a catalyst, not a reactant.
  16. Equilibrium constant expressed in terms of pressure is K_p; related to K_c by K_p = K_c(RT)^Δn.
  17. Increasing temperature shifts an exothermic equilibrium towards reactants.
  18. Surface area effect is most pronounced for solid–gas reactions.
  19. Arrhenius plot (ln k vs 1/T) yields a straight line whose slope = –E_a/R.
  20. In the decomposition of KClO₃, MnO₂ acts as a catalyst.

🔢 Formula / Key Data Box

Rate Law: Rate = k [A]^m [B]^n …

Arrhenius Equation: k = A e^(–Eₐ/RT)

Half‑life (first order): t½ = 0.693 / k

Equilibrium Constant (K_c): K_c = ([C]^c [D]^d)/([A]^a [B]^b)

Relation K_p – K_c: K_p = K_c (RT)^Δn

Le Chatelier Rules: ↑T → endothermic side; ↑P → fewer gas moles side; ↑[Reactant] → forward shift; catalyst → faster attainment.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • Assuming the reaction order equals stoichiometric coefficient – order must be derived experimentally.
  • Thinking a catalyst changes the position of equilibrium – it only speeds the approach.
  • Confusing ΔH sign with the direction of shift under temperature change.
  • Using concentration units in K_p calculations without converting to partial pressures.
  • Neglecting the effect of change in total moles (Δn) when converting K_c to K_p.
  • Applying Le Chatelier’s principle to non‑gaseous systems without considering activity coefficients.
  • Ignoring the steric factor, leading to overestimation of rate from collision frequency alone.
  • Treating a reversible reaction as irreversible in ICE tables, causing wrong equilibrium concentrations.
