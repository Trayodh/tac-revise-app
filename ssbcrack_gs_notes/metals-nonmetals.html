📖 Complete Theory

1. Fundamental Differences

Metals are characterised by a sea of delocalised electrons that give rise to metallic bonding. This electron cloud explains their high electrical and thermal conductivity, malleability and ductility. Non‑metals, on the other hand, have localized electrons, leading to covalent or ionic bonds, and consequently they are generally poor conductors and brittle.

2. Physical Properties – A Comparative View

Key physical properties are summarised in the table below. Notice the inverse relationship between ionisation energy and metallic character.

Property Metals Non‑Metals
State at 25 °C Solid (except Hg) Solid, liquid, gas
Luster Shiny, metallic Dull or non‑metallic
Conductivity Good (electric & thermal) Poor (except graphite)
Melting Point Generally high Low to moderate (C is high)
Density > 5 g cm⁻³ (except Li, Mg) < 5 g cm⁻³ (except C)

3. Chemical Reactivity Trends

Reactivity of metals increases down Group 1 (Li → Cs) because the outermost electron feels less nuclear attraction. In Group 2, the +2 oxidation state is stable, but the reactivity is lower than Group 1 due to higher ionisation energy. Transition metals display variable oxidation states, which is the basis for many coloured compounds and catalytic applications.

Halogens (Group 17) are the most electronegative elements. Their reactivity diminishes down the group as the atomic radius increases and electron‑electron repulsion in the valence shell grows.

4. Oxidation, Corrosion and Passivation

Corrosion is a redox process where a metal loses electrons to an oxidising agent (usually O₂ or H₂O). The classic example is the rusting of iron:

4Fe + 3O₂ + 6H₂O → 4Fe(OH)₃ → Fe₂O₃·nH₂O

Passivation, observed in Al and Ti, forms a thin, adherent oxide layer that blocks further attack. This property is exploited in aircraft skins and naval hulls.

5. Alloys – Tailoring Properties for Defence

Alloying is the art of mixing a base metal with one or more elements to achieve desired mechanical or chemical traits.

  • Steel (Fe‑C): Carbon (0.2‑2 %) increases hardness; addition of Cr, Ni, Mo yields stainless, high‑strength variants.
  • Aluminium alloys (Al‑Cu, Al‑Zn‑Mg): Low density and high strength → aircraft frames, UAVs.
  • Titanium alloys (Ti‑Al‑V): Excellent strength‑to‑weight ratio, corrosion resistance → naval submarines and missile casings.
  • Bronze (Cu‑Sn): Superior wear resistance, used in artillery breech rings.

6. Non‑Metals in Defence Chemistry

Key non‑metals play strategic roles:

  • Nitrogen: Base of high‑energy explosives (RDX, HMX) and propellants (ammonium nitrate).
  • Oxygen: Essential for combustion; liquid O₂ used in rocket oxidisers.
  • Fluorine: Forms UF₆ for uranium enrichment (nuclear propulsion).
  • Carbon: Graphite as a neutron moderator; carbon‑fiber composites for lightweight armour.
  • Sulphur: Component of smokeless powders and some high‑energy fuels.

7. Quantitative Aspects – Ionisation Energy & Lattice Energy

Ionisation energy (IE) trends explain why Group 1 metals readily lose electrons, while halogens have high electron‑affinity (EA). Lattice energy (U) for ionic solids follows the Born‑Landé equation; higher charge and smaller ionic radii give larger U, resulting in higher melting points and hardness. For example, NaCl (U ≈ 787 kJ mol⁻¹) vs. KCl (U ≈ 701 kJ mol⁻¹).

8. Environmental & Safety Considerations

Heavy metals (Pb, Hg, Cd) pose toxicity risks; modern defence equipment now prefers lead‑free primers and mercury‑free switches. Recycling of aluminium and steel from de‑commissioned platforms reduces ecological footprint and saves strategic material.

📚 Important Definitions

Term Definition
Metallic Bonding A type of chemical bond where valence electrons are delocalised over a lattice of positively charged ions.
Passivation Formation of a thin, protective oxide layer on a metal surface that prevents further corrosion.
Alloy A mixture of a base metal with one or more additional elements to improve properties.
Electropositivity Tendency of an atom to donate electrons and form positive ions.
Electronegativity Measure of an atom's ability to attract electrons towards itself in a bond.
Corrosion Destructive oxidation of a metal caused by reaction with its environment.
Lattice Energy Energy released when gaseous ions combine to form an ionic solid.
Oxidation State Formal charge an atom would have if all bonds were ionic.
Malleability Ability of a metal to be hammered into thin sheets without breaking.
Ductility Ability of a metal to be drawn into wires.

⭐ Important Facts (Defence Exam Favourites)

  1. Aluminium is the most widely used metal in aircraft due to its low density and good strength.
  2. Titanium’s modulus of elasticity is about 2‑3 times that of steel while being 40 % lighter.
  3. Group 1 metals react violently with water, producing H₂ gas and metal hydroxide.
  4. Iron forms Fe³⁺ and Fe²⁺ ions; Fe³⁺ is more stable in oxidising environments.
  5. Stainless steel contains at least 10.5 % chromium which forms a passive Cr₂O₃ layer.
  6. Mercury is the only metal that is liquid at room temperature.
  7. Carbon in the form of graphite conducts electricity along the planes of its layers.
  8. Halogens form diatomic molecules (F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂) in their elemental state.
  9. Lead‑free primers are now mandatory in Indian armed forces for environmental safety.
  10. RDX (C₃H₆N₆O₆) is a high‑explosive derived from nitro‑group chemistry of non‑metals.
  11. Nickel‑based super‑alloys retain strength at > 1000 °C, crucial for jet‑engine turbines.
  12. Zn coating on steel (galvanisation) reduces corrosion rate by up to 90 %.
  13. Silicon is added to aluminium to improve castability and reduce oxidation.
  14. Phosphorus is a de‑oxidiser in steel making, removing dissolved oxygen.
  15. Gold’s high conductivity and inertness make it ideal for high‑frequency connectors.
  16. Copper’s antimicrobial property is used in hospital‑grade military field kits.
  17. Fe‑Al intermetallics are being researched for ultra‑light armour.
  18. Uranium hexafluoride (UF₆) is the gaseous compound used in isotope enrichment.
  19. Magnesium burns with a bright white flame, useful for flares and illumination.
  20. Electro‑plating of zinc on steel is called “galvanising”, named after Luigi Galvani.

NDA often includes numerical problems – calculating the mass loss due to corrosion, or determining the percent composition of an alloy.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • Confusing “electropositivity” with “electronegativity”. Remember: metals → low EN, high EP.
  • Assuming all non‑metals are insulators – graphite conducts electricity.
  • Mixing up the order of halogen reactivity; reactivity decreases down the group.
  • Ignoring the effect of alloying elements on corrosion; e.g., Cr in steel forms a protective layer.
  • Over‑generalising that all transition metals are magnetic – only certain ones (Fe, Co, Ni) are ferromagnetic.
  • Writing the formula for rust as FeO₂; the correct representation is Fe₂O₃·nH₂O.
  • Forgetting that Zn is more electropositive than Fe, which is why it sacrificially corrodes.
  • Treating “metallic” as synonymous with “conductive”; mercury is metallic but a poor conductor compared to Cu.
