📖 Complete Theory

1. Nature of Waves

Waves are disturbances that travel through a medium (or vacuum for EM waves) carrying energy. In the defence context, mechanical waves (e.g., sound) are crucial for sonar, communication, and detection. Mechanical waves require a material medium – air, water, or solids – and can be longitudinal (particles oscillate parallel to propagation) or transverse (particles oscillate perpendicular).

2. Fundamental Wave Parameters

The five basic parameters are:

  • Wavelength (λ): distance between successive identical points (e.g., crest‑to‑crest).
  • Frequency (f): number of cycles per second (Hz).
  • Period (T): time for one cycle (T = 1/f).
  • Speed (v): distance covered per unit time (v = fλ).
  • Amplitude (A): maximum displacement from equilibrium; determines loudness for sound.

3. Sound as a Longitudinal Wave

When a source vibrates, it compresses and rarefies the surrounding air molecules, creating regions of high and low pressure that travel outward. The speed of sound (v) in a gas is given by:

v = √(γ R T / M)

where γ is the adiabatic index, R the universal gas constant, T the absolute temperature, and M the molar mass. In air at 20 °C, v ≈ 343 m s⁻¹.

4. Intensity and Sound Level

Intensity (I) is power per unit area (W m⁻²). Human hearing perceives intensity logarithmically, expressed as sound level β in decibels (dB):

β = 10 log₁₀(I / I₀)  where I₀ = 10⁻¹² W m⁻²

Key thresholds: 0 dB (threshold of hearing), 60 dB (normal conversation), 120 dB (threshold of pain).

5. Interference & Resonance

When two waves meet, they superpose. If their phases align (Δϕ = 0, 2π,…), they produce constructive interference (amplitude adds). If they are out of phase (Δϕ = π, 3π,…), they cause destructive interference (amplitude reduces). Resonance occurs when a system is driven at its natural frequency, leading to large amplitude oscillations – a principle exploited in artillery range finders and musical instruments.

6. Doppler Effect

The Doppler Effect describes the change in observed frequency (f) when source and observer move relative to each other. For a source moving with speed vₛ towards a stationary observer:

f = f₀ · (v / (v – vₛ))

For an observer moving with speed vₒ towards a stationary source:

f = f₀ · ((v + vₒ) / v)

When both move, combine the two effects. The shift is upward (higher pitch) when they approach and downward when they recede. This principle is vital for radar speed detection, sonar target tracking, and even for estimating the speed of enemy aircraft from their engine noise.

7. Applications in Defence

  • Sonar: Active sonar emits a pulse, measures echo time to locate submarines. Passive sonar listens for enemy noises, using Doppler shift to estimate speed.
  • Radar: Though electromagnetic, the underlying wave principles (reflection, Doppler) are analogous. Radar‑derived speed uses the same formula with c (speed of light).
  • Ultrasonic Imaging: High‑frequency sound (>20 kHz) penetrates tissues, reflecting off interfaces to form images – used in battlefield medics.
  • Acoustic Camouflage: Submarines apply anechoic tiles to absorb incident sound, reducing reflected echo intensity.
  • Communication: HF radio uses ionospheric reflection; understanding wave propagation aids secure, long‑range links.

⭐ Important Facts (Defence Exam Favourites)

  1. Speed of sound in dry air at 0 °C is 331 m s⁻¹.
  2. Sound travels fastest in solids, slowest in gases.
  3. Frequency of a middle‑C note is ≈ 261.6 Hz.
  4. Human audible range: 20 Hz – 20 kHz.
  5. Ultrasound is used for submarine detection because water attenuates high‑frequency sound less than low‑frequency.
  6. Decibel scale is logarithmic; a 10 dB increase means ten times the intensity.
  7. Threshold of pain is about 120 dB.
  8. Active sonar pulse duration is typically a few milliseconds.
  9. Passive sonar can detect a ship’s propeller noise from >20 km under favourable conditions.
  10. Doppler shift formula for moving source: f = f₀ · (v / (v – vₛ)).
  11. For a moving observer: f = f₀ · ((v + vₒ) / v).
  12. Radar speed guns use the Doppler shift of microwave reflections.
  13. Speed of sound increases by ≈ 0.6 m s⁻¹ per °C rise in temperature.
  14. Acoustic impedance mismatch causes reflection at interfaces.
  15. Resonance frequency of a closed pipe: f = nv/4L (n = odd integer).
  16. Sound intensity level of a whisper is about 30 dB.
  17. Sound level of a jet engine at 100 m is ≈ 110 dB.
  18. Echo‑location used by bats is analogous to sonar.
  19. Echo‑time for a target 1 km away (round‑trip) ≈ 1.3 s in water.
  20. “An echo‑free zone” around a submarine is achieved with anechoic tiles.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • Sign error in Doppler formula – forgetting “‑” for a source moving away.
  • Using speed of sound in air for water‑based sonar problems.
  • Mixing up intensity (I) with sound level (β); dB requires logarithm.
  • Assuming constructive interference always doubles amplitude – it adds vectorially.
  • Neglecting temperature correction for speed of sound calculations.
  • Confusing active and passive sonar capabilities, especially regarding stealth.
  • Treating sound as an electromagnetic wave and applying c instead of the appropriate medium speed.
  • Incorrect unit conversion when frequencies are given in kHz but formulas need Hz.
