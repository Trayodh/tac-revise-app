📖 Complete Theory

1. Overview of the Human Skeleton

The human skeleton is a dynamic framework that provides support, protects vital organs, and serves as the attachment site for muscles. It is divided into two major parts:

  • Axial Skeleton – 80 bones forming the central axis: skull (22), vertebral column (26), ribs & sternum (25).
  • Appendicular Skeleton – 126 bones that form the limbs and their girdles: shoulder girdle (clavicle + scapula), pelvic girdle (ilium, ischium, pubis), and the 30 bones of each upper and lower limb.

In a newborn there are about 270 bones; many fuse during growth (e.g., the sacrum, coccyx, and the cranial sutures), resulting in 206 bones in the adult.

2. Bone Structure and Classification

Every bone consists of two layers:

  • Compact bone – dense outer layer providing strength.
  • Spongy (cancellous) bone – porous interior filled with red marrow.

Based on shape, bones are classified as:

Type Examples Key Feature
Long Femur, Humerus Diaphysis + epiphyses; mainly for leverage.
Short Carpals, Tarsals Cuboidal shape; provide stability.
Flat Scapula, Sternum Protection & muscle attachment.
Irregular Vertebrae, Facial bones Complex shapes for specialised functions.

3. Bone Growth & Remodeling

Bone lengthening occurs at the epiphyseal (growth) plates via endochondral ossification. Hormones such as growth hormone (GH), thyroid hormone, and sex steroids accelerate this process during puberty. Simultaneously, bone remodeling constantly renews the skeleton: osteoclasts resorb old bone, while osteoblasts** lay down new matrix. The balance is essential; imbalance leads to osteoporosis or osteopetrosis.

4. Joints – Types and Functions

Joints (articulations) are classified on two bases: structure (fibrous, cartilaginous, synovial) and function (synarthrosis – immovable, amphiarthrosis – slightly movable, diarthrosis – freely movable). The most important for defence‑related movement are synovial joints such as the shoulder, hip, knee, and elbow.

Key components of a synovial joint:

  • Articular cartilage – smooth hyaline cartilage covering bone ends.
  • Joint capsule – fibrous tissue enclosing the joint.
  • Synovial membrane – secretes lubricating fluid.
  • Ligaments – reinforce the capsule and limit excessive motion.
  • Meniscus (in knee) – shock absorber made of fibrocartilage.

5. Muscle Tissue – Types and Structure

Three muscle types exist:

Muscle Type Striation Control Location
Skeletal Yes Voluntary Attached to bones via tendons.
Cardiac Yes Involuntary Heart wall.
Smooth No Involuntary Walls of hollow organs.

6. Sliding Filament Theory of Contraction

When a motor neuron fires, acetylcholine (ACh) is released at the neuromuscular junction (NMJ). This depolarises the muscle fibre membrane, triggering an action potential that travels down the T‑tubules and releases Ca²⁺ from the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Calcium binds to troponin, causing a conformational shift in tropomyosin** that exposes the myosin‑binding sites on actin.

Myosin heads, energized by ATP hydrolysis, attach to actin forming cross‑bridges, pull the actin filament inward (power stroke), release ADP + Pi, and then detach when a new ATP binds. Repeating cycles shorten the sarcomere, producing muscle tension.

Analogy: Think of a rowing crew (myosin heads) pulling a rope (actin) in rhythm; the boat (muscle fibre) moves forward as each rower repeats the pull‑release cycle.

7. Muscle Energy Sources & Fatigue

During short bursts (≤5 s) muscles use stored ATP and phosphocreatine. For activities lasting 30 s–2 min, glycolysis (anaerobic) dominates, producing lactic acid. Longer activities (>2 min) rely on aerobic metabolism (oxidative phosphorylation) using glucose, fatty acids, and amino acids.

Fatigue arises from depletion of phosphocreatine, accumulation of inorganic phosphate, and drop in pH due to lactic acid, all of which impair cross‑bridge cycling.

8. Clinical & Military Relevance

Understanding the skeletal‑muscular system is vital for:

  • Assessing combat‑related injuries (fractures, sprains, muscle strains).
  • Designing load‑bearing equipment (backpacks, exoskeletons).
  • Optimising training regimes for endurance, speed, and strength.
  • Implementing rehabilitation protocols after injuries.
  • Evaluating ergonomics of weapons handling and shooting posture.

⭐ Important Facts (Defence Exam Favourites)

  1. The adult human skeleton has 206 bones.
  2. Femur is the longest and strongest bone in the body.
  3. The humerus forms the upper arm and articulates with the scapula at the glenoid cavity.
  4. Vertebral column consists of 7 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral (fused) and 4 coccygeal (fused) vertebrae.
  5. The clavicle is the only long bone that lies horizontally.
  6. Synovial joints are the most movable type of joint.
  7. Articular cartilage is composed of hyaline cartilage and contains no blood vessels.
  8. Osteoblasts secrete osteoid, which later mineralises to become bone.
  9. Osteoclasts are multinucleated cells derived from monocyte‑macrophage lineage.
  10. Growth plates close after puberty under the influence of sex hormones.
  11. Parathyroid hormone raises blood calcium, while calcitonin lowers it.
  12. Muscle contraction is triggered by the release of acetylcholine at the NMJ.
  13. Calcium binds to troponin, moving tropomyosin away from actin’s myosin‑binding sites.
  14. ATP is required for both the power stroke and detachment of myosin heads.
  15. Type I muscle fibres are rich in mitochondria and myoglobin.
  16. Type IIb fibres fatigue quickly but generate high force.
  17. The knee joint is a modified hinge joint with menisci for shock absorption.
  18. Rotator cuff injuries are common in soldiers due to repetitive overhead activities.
  19. Bone remodeling takes about 3‑4 months for a complete cycle.
  20. Excessive cortisol can lead to bone loss (osteoporosis).

Past‑year questions from CDS, AFCAT and NDA show a steady focus on the skeletal‑muscular system, with about 12‑15 % of the total biology questions coming from this chapter. The most recurring sub‑topics are:

  • Identification of major bones (skull, vertebrae, femur, tibia).

  • Bone remodeling hormones – PTH vs. calcitonin questions.

Common traps include confusing the functions of osteoblasts and osteoclasts, or mixing up the locations of articular vs. fibrocartilage. Paying attention to the “type of joint” attached to a specific bone (e.g., shoulder = ball‑and‑socket) helps avoid such pitfalls.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • Confusing the number of vertebrae with the number of spinal regions – remember 7 C, 12 T, 5 L.
  • Mixing up osteoblast (bone‑forming) with osteoclast (bone‑resorbing) functions.
  • Assuming all joints are synovial; many are fibrous (e.g., sutures).
  • Attributing calcium release to the sarcoplasmic reticulum without mentioning the role of the T‑tubules.
  • Neglecting that smooth muscle is non‑striated – a common error in MCQs.
  • Over‑generalising that all fractures are transverse; remember spiral and comminuted types.
  • Forgetting that the clavicle is the only long bone placed horizontally.
  • Ignoring the effect of sex hormones on epiphyseal plate closure.
