📖 Complete Theory

The endocrine system is a network of specialised glands that secrete chemical messengers called hormones. Unlike the exocrine system, which uses ducts to deliver its products to body surfaces, endocrine glands are ductless and release hormones directly into the bloodstream. These hormones travel to distant target organs where they bind to specific receptors, initiating a cascade of intracellular events that modify cellular activity.

2. Hormone Action Mechanisms

Hormones are classified by their solubility and receptor type:

  • Peptide/Protein hormones (e.g., insulin, GH) bind to cell‑surface receptors → activate G‑protein coupled pathways → second messengers (cAMP, IP₃/DAG).
  • Steroid hormones (e.g., cortisol, estrogen) cross the lipid membrane, bind intracellular receptors → hormone‑receptor complex acts as a transcription factor → gene expression changes.
  • Amines (e.g., thyroid hormones, catecholamines) have mixed mechanisms; thyroid hormones behave like steroids, catecholamines act via surface receptors.

3. Feedback Control

The endocrine system largely operates on negative feedback. An increase in a hormone’s level suppresses its own release (e.g., high cortisol inhibits CRH and ACTH). Positive feedback is rare but crucial in events like childbirth (oxytocin surge) and the LH surge leading to ovulation.

4. Clinical Disorders – A Defence Perspective

Understanding endocrine pathologies is vital for medical fitness of defence personnel. Key disorders include:

  • Hyperthyroidism – weight loss, tachycardia, heat intolerance; can impair endurance.
  • Hypothyroidism – fatigue, bradycardia, myopathy; may affect operational readiness.
  • Diabetes Mellitus (Type‑1 & Type‑2) – chronic hyperglycaemia; risk of hypoglycaemia during high‑intensity missions.
  • Cushing’s Syndrome – excess cortisol; causes muscle wasting, hypertension, poor wound healing.
  • Addison’s Disease – adrenal insufficiency; leads to hypotension, electrolyte imbalance.
  • Growth Hormone Deficiency/Excess – dwarfism vs acromegaly; both affect physical performance.

5. Hormone‑Related Stress Response in Combat

During combat, the “adrenal axis” releases catecholamines and cortisol, raising heart rate, mobilising glucose, and dampening non‑essential functions (digestion, immunity). Prolonged activation can cause “burn‑out” – a reason why endocrine monitoring is part of elite soldier health programmes.

🧠 Memory Tricks & Mnemonics

  • “HAPPY GUTS” – Hypothalamus, Adrenal (cortex), Pituitary, Pancreas, Y (thyroid), Gonads, U (parathyroid), T (thyroid), S (adrenal medulla). Remember the “happy guts” of a soldier during stress.
  • “CATS” for adrenal cortex hormones – Cortisol, Aldosterone, Testosterone (androgens), Steroids (overall).
  • “TIC‑TOC” – T3/T4 (thyroid), Insulin, Cortisol – “Tic‑toc” of the body’s clock; when any stops, the body falters.
  • “FIGHT‑OR‑FLEE” – Catecholamines (Epi/Norepi) = “F‑O‑F”. Visualise a soldier shouting “F‑O‑F!” before a raid.
  • “GO‑NO‑GO” – Goitre (↑ TSH) = GO; Neutral (normal) = NO; Go (hyperthyroid) = GO. Helps decide treatment direction.

⭐ Important Facts (Defence Exam Favourites)

  1. Hypothalamus releases TRH which stimulates TSH from the anterior pituitary.
  2. Thyroid hormones increase basal metabolic rate by up‑regulating Na⁺/K⁺‑ATPase.
  3. Cortisol follows a diurnal rhythm – peak at 8 am, trough at midnight.
  4. Insulin deficiency leads to ketoacidosis, a dangerous condition in high‑altitude missions.
  5. Adrenal medulla is a modified sympathetic ganglion.
  6. Parathyroid hormone raises serum calcium by stimulating osteoclasts.
  7. Excess aldosterone causes hypokalemia – can precipitate muscle cramps in soldiers.
  8. Oxytocin is released during childbirth and also during bonding – important for morale.
  9. GH secretion is pulsatile and highest during deep sleep.
  10. Hyperthyroidism can cause atrial fibrillation, a risk for pilots.
  11. Low TSH with high T3/T4 indicates primary hyperthyroidism.
  12. ACTH stimulates cortisol; excess ACTH → Cushing’s disease.
  13. Glucagon acts via G‑protein → cAMP → glycogenolysis.
  14. Somatostatin inhibits both GH and insulin release.
  15. Male soldiers with low testosterone may show reduced aggression – a factor in selection.
  16. Thyroid calcitonin lowers blood calcium but is clinically insignificant in humans.
  17. ADH acts on V2 receptors in the collecting duct to insert aquaporin‑2.
  18. Stress‑induced cortisol can suppress the immune system – important for infection risk in camps.
  19. Hyperparathyroidism leads to “stones, bones, groans, and psychiatric overtones”.
  20. Acute adrenal crisis (Addisonian crisis) is a medical emergency on the battlefield.

🔢 Formula / Key Data Box

Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR) Approximation: BMR ≈ 70 × (Body weight in kg)^0.75 kcal/day.

Free Thyroxine (FT4) Conversion: FT4 (ng/dL) × 12.9 = FT4 (pmol/L).

Cortisol Stress Index: (Morning cortisol ÷ Evening cortisol) × 100. Normal > 150%.

Insulin Sensitivity (HOMA‑IR): (Fasting insulin µU/mL × Fasting glucose mg/dL) / 405.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • Confusing the synthesis site (hypothalamus) with the storage site (posterior pituitary) for ADH and oxytocin.
  • Assuming that all adrenal hormones are catecholamines; forgetting that cortisol is a steroid.
  • Mixing up the effects of PTH and calcitonin – remember PTH raises Ca²⁺, calcitonin lowers it.
  • Over‑looking that thyroid hormones are stored as thyroglobulin‑bound in the gland.
  • Interpreting a low TSH as hypothyroidism – it actually indicates hyperthyroidism.
  • Neglecting the role of cortisol in suppressing the immune system, leading to infection risk in field conditions.
  • Assuming that “stress” always means psychological; in physiology it primarily triggers catecholamine release.
