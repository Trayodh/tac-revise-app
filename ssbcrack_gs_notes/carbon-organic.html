📖 Complete Theory

Carbon, the backbone of organic chemistry, possesses a unique ability to catenate – form long chains or rings with itself. This is due to its four valence electrons and the formation of covalent sigma (σ) bonds. Depending on the hybridisation of the carbon atom, the geometry and bond type change, giving rise to diverse families of compounds.

1. Hybridisation of Carbon

When one 2s and three 2p orbitals mix, an sp³ hybrid set is produced – tetrahedral arrangement (109.5°). Example: methane (CH₄). In alkenes, one p‑orbital remains unhybridised, forming a π‑bond; the carbon is sp² hybridised (120°). Alkynes involve two unhybridised p‑orbitals forming a σ‑bond and two π‑bonds; the carbon is sp hybridised (180°). Hybridisation dictates bond strength, length, and reactivity.

2. Hydrocarbon Classification

Saturated hydrocarbons (alkanes) contain only single C–C bonds. They follow the general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ and are relatively inert. Unsaturated hydrocarbons include alkenes (CₙH₂ₙ) with one or more C=C double bonds and alkynes (CₙH₂ₙ₋₂) with C≡C triple bonds. These possess π‑bonds that are electrophilic, making them more reactive. Aromatic hydrocarbons are cyclic, planar, conjugated systems obeying Huckel’s rule (4n+2 π‑electrons). Benzene (C₆H₆) is the prototype.

3. Isomerism in Hydrocarbons

Isomers have the same molecular formula but differ in connectivity or spatial arrangement. Structural isomers differ in carbon skeleton (e.g., n‑butane vs iso‑butane). Geometric isomers arise from restricted rotation around double bonds (cis‑ vs trans‑). Optical isomers (enantiomers) occur when a molecule contains a chiral centre – a carbon attached to four different groups.

4. Polymerisation – The Giant Molecules

Polymers are macromolecules formed by repetitive linking of monomer units. Two principal mechanisms dominate:

  • Addition (chain‑growth) polymerisation: The monomer’s double bond opens, adding to a growing chain without eliminating any small molecule. Initiators (radicals, cations, anions) start the reaction. Example: polymerisation of ethylene → polyethylene (PE).
  • Condensation (step‑growth) polymerisation: Two monomers combine, releasing a by‑product (usually water or methanol). The reaction proceeds through successive condensation steps. Example: condensation of adipic acid and hexamethylenediamine → Nylon‑6,6.

The degree of polymerisation (DP) dictates molecular weight (Mₙ = DP × M₀). High DP gives stronger, tougher materials – essential for aerospace and armour.

6. Physical Properties & Their Defence Relevance

Thermoplastics can be reheated, reshaped, and recycled – vital for field repairs. Thermosets, once cured, become rigid and heat‑resistant – used in composite armour. Carbon‑Fiber‑Reinforced Polymers (CFRP) combine high tensile strength with low weight, ideal for UAV airframes and stealth technology. Understanding degradation (UV, oxidative) helps in maintenance planning for equipment exposed to harsh environments.

7. Environmental & Safety Aspects

Micro‑plastics pose ecological hazards; the defence sector is moving towards biodegradable polymers (PLA, PHA) for temporary structures. Proper disposal and recycling protocols are now part of the logistics manuals for forward bases.

8. Quick Calculations – Example

Problem: Find the mass of polymer obtained from 10 g of ethylene (C₂H₄) assuming 95 % conversion to polyethylene (PE, repeat unit –CH₂–CH₂–, M₀ = 28 g mol⁻¹).

Moles of ethylene = 10 g / 28 g mol⁻¹ = 0.357 mol. DP = 1000 (typical for HDPE). Mₙ = 1000 × 28 = 28 000 g mol⁻¹. Mass of polymer = 0.357 mol × 28 000 g mol⁻¹ × 0.95 ≈ 9.5 kg.

  • “SP³ = 3‑SOLDIERS, SP² = 2‑GUNS, SP = 1‑SNIPER” – Remember hybridisation by the number of remaining p‑orbitals.
  • “C‑H‑A‑R‑M” for Hydrocarbon families:
    C – Carbon chains (Alkanes),
    H – H‑double‑H (Alkenes),
    A – Alkyne,
    R – Ring (Aromatics),
    M – Macromolecule (Polymers).
  • “PE = “P” for “Protect” soldiers, “E” for “Equipment”” – Helps recall that Polyethylene is widely used in protective gear.
  • “Nylon‑6,6 = 6‑by‑6 = 6 months of training, 6 weeks of field‑use” – Connects the two 6’s to the dual‑step condensation of adipic acid and hexamethylenediamine.

  • “Benzene = “B”ombardier’s “E”ight‑membered “N”etwork “Z”ero “E”lectron‑pair loss” – Reminds that benzene has a stable 6‑π‑electron system.

  • Alkyne: CₙH₂ₙ₋₂

Polymerisation Equations

  • Addition: n CH₂=CH₂ → –[CH₂–CH₂]ₙ– (Polyethylene)

Degree of Polymerisation (DP)

  • DP = Mₙ / M₀
  • Mₙ = number‑average molecular weight; M₀ = repeat‑unit mass.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • Confusing sp² with sp³ hybridisation – remember the number of remaining p‑orbitals.
  • Using CₙH₂ₙ formula for alkynes – alkynes are CₙH₂ₙ₋₂.
  • Assuming all polymers are thermoplastics – thermosets exist and are crucial for heat‑resistant composites.
  • Neglecting the by‑product in condensation polymerisation – leads to wrong mass balance calculations.
  • Mixing up aromatic stability with conjugation alone – both planarity and 4n+2 π‑electrons are required.
  • Overlooking chirality in simple molecules – a carbon with four different groups makes the molecule optically active.
  • Treating “polyethylene” and “polypropylene” as interchangeable – their side groups dictate distinct properties.
