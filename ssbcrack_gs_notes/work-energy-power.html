📖 Complete Theory

The concepts of work, energy and power form the backbone of classical mechanics. They are not merely algebraic formulas; they describe how forces cause motion and how that motion can be quantified. The chapter begins with the definition of work. When a constant force **F** acts on a body and displaces it through a distance **s** at an angle **θ** to the direction of the force, the work done is

W = F·s·cosθ (Joules)

If the force varies with displacement, the work is the area under the F–s curve, i.e., W = ∫F·ds. This integral form is crucial for solving problems involving springs, non‑linear resistive forces, or variable gravity (e.g., near a planet).

Kinetic and Potential Energy

A body of mass **m** moving with speed **v** possesses kinetic energy (KE) given by KE = ½ mv². The quadratic dependence on speed explains why high‑speed projectiles carry enormous energy even if their mass is modest.

Potential energy (PE) is stored energy due to position or configuration. The most common forms are gravitational (PEg = mgh) and elastic (PEe = ½ kx²). Gravitational PE is a conservative quantity; the work done by gravity around any closed path is zero. Elastic PE is also conservative if the spring obeys Hooke’s law.

Work‑Energy Theorem

When a net external force **Fnet** acts on a body, the total work done equals the change in kinetic energy:

Wnet = ΔKE = KEfinal – KEinitial

This theorem bypasses the need to solve Newton’s second law for every problem. For example, a soldier sliding down a frictionless slope gains kinetic energy equal to the loss in gravitational potential energy.

Conservation of Mechanical Energy

If only conservative forces act, the sum of kinetic and potential energy remains constant:

KE + PE = constant

In real‑world scenarios, friction or air drag introduces non‑conservative work, leading to mechanical energy loss. In defence problems, this loss often appears as heat or sound, and the question may ask for the fraction of energy retained.

Power

Power quantifies how fast work is done. The scalar definition is P = W/t. For a body moving with velocity **v** under a force **F**, power can also be expressed as:

P = F·v·cosθ

In a tank’s engine, the power rating (in kW) tells how quickly the engine can convert chemical energy to kinetic energy of the vehicle.

Momentum and Collisions

Momentum **p = mv** is a vector quantity conserved in any closed system, regardless of the nature of internal forces. In a collision, the total momentum before equals the total momentum after:

∑pinitial = ∑pfinal

A collision is called elastic if kinetic energy is also conserved; otherwise, it is inelastic. For perfectly inelastic collisions, the bodies stick together, and the final speed is given by:

v = (m₁v₁ + m₂v₂)/(m₁ + m₂)

The coefficient of restitution (e) characterises the ‘bounciness’: e = 1 for a perfectly elastic impact, e = 0 for a perfectly inelastic one.

Practical Examples

  • Projectile Launch: A 5 kg artillery shell fired at 300 m s⁻¹ carries KE = ½·5·300² = 2.25 × 10⁵ J.
  • Tank Track: If a tank climbs a 30° incline of 10 m height, the work done against gravity is mgh = 25 000·9.8·10 ≈ 2.45 MJ, which must be supplied by the engine within a given time to compute power.
  • Bullet‑target interaction: An almost elastic collision between a bullet and a steel plate (e ≈ 0.9) helps determine the rebound speed of the bullet.

Key Take‑aways

- Always check whether forces are conservative before applying mechanical‑energy conservation.
- Write down momentum equations first in collision problems; then check KE conservation if the collision is elastic.
- Use the coefficient of restitution to relate speeds before and after impact.
- Power = Force × Velocity is handy for engine‑performance questions.

  • “E = ½ mv²” = “Half‑Mighty V‑Squad”: Visualise a squad of soldiers (mass) moving at velocity V; half of them carry the energy.
  • “REST‑e” for Restitution: “R‑E‑S‑T‑E‑T‑I‑O‑N – Every Soldier Trains In Optimal N‑orms” – the word “REST” reminds you of bounce.
  • “F‑V‑P” = “Force‑Velocity‑Power”: In a tank, think of “F‑V‑P = Fast‑Vehicle‑Power” to link the formula P = F·v.
  • “M‑C‑E” (Momentum‑Conservation‑Elastic): “Mission‑Control‑Engineers” – if the mission control says “keep momentum”, you know it’s an elastic scenario.
  • “G‑R‑A‑V‑E” (Gravity‑Rises‑After‑Velocity‑Energy): The letters spell “GRAVE”; a falling object’s potential energy becomes kinetic – a grave transformation.

⭐ Important Facts (Defence Exam Favourites)

  1. Work is a scalar; it can be positive, negative or zero.
  2. 1 J = 1 N·m = 1 kg·m²·s⁻².
  3. KE is always positive; zero only when the object is at rest.
  4. Mechanical energy is conserved only in the absence of non‑conservative forces.
  5. Power measured in kilowatts (kW) is commonly used for engine ratings.
  6. The coefficient of restitution is dimensionless.
  7. For a perfectly elastic head‑on collision of equal masses, velocities exchange.
  8. In a perfectly inelastic collision, bodies stick together and move with common velocity.
  9. Momentum is a vector; direction matters in 2‑D collisions.
  10. Friction does negative work, converting mechanical energy to heat.
  11. Gravitational potential energy near Earth’s surface is linear with height.
  12. Elastic potential energy is quadratic with spring compression/extension.
  13. Work done by a constant force at 90° to displacement is zero.
  14. Power can also be expressed as P = τ·ω for rotating systems.
  15. Energy of a 5 kg shell at 300 m s⁻¹ ≈ 2.25 × 10⁵ J.
  16. Coefficient of restitution for a steel‑rubber ball ≈ 0.85.
  17. Maximum kinetic energy transfer occurs when m₁ = m₂ in a collision.
  18. In missile launch, most chemical energy becomes kinetic and potential energy of the projectile.
  19. Work‑energy theorem is a shortcut to avoid solving differential equations.
  20. Power = Force × Velocity is especially useful for jet‑engine thrust calculations.

📝 PYQ Pattern Analysis

Past five years of CDS, AFCAT and NDA papers show a steady presence of energy‑based questions. Approximately 70% of the physics sections contain at least one problem on work‑energy or collisions. The most frequent sub‑topics are:

  • Work‑energy theorem applied to inclined planes.
  • Conservation of mechanical energy with frictional losses.
  • Momentum conservation in 1‑D collisions, often with coefficient of restitution.
  • Power calculations for engines or artillery.

Recent trends indicate a shift toward multi‑step problems where candidates must first compute work, then convert it to kinetic energy, and finally evaluate power. Questions now frequently embed real‑world contexts – e.g., “A tank climbs a hill”, “A bullet hits a steel plate”. Common traps include ignoring the sign of work done by friction, mixing up KE and PE formulas, and treating e as a speed rather than a ratio.

🎓 Examination Perspective

AFCAT: Focuses on quick calculations. Look for “Find the power output of a jet engine” or “Calculate the speed after a perfectly elastic collision”. Time‑saving shortcuts are rewarded.

NDA: Mixes theory with application. Frequently asks for the derivation of the work‑energy theorem and then a numerical problem on a projectile’s kinetic energy at the apex.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • Using g = 10 m s⁻² everywhere – remember the exam may require 9.8 m s⁻² for higher precision.
  • Ignoring the angle factor cosθ in work calculations; a force perpendicular to displacement does zero work.
  • Assuming kinetic energy is conserved in any collision; only elastic collisions satisfy this.
  • Mixing up the sign of the coefficient of restitution; e is always positive.
  • Treating friction as a conservative force – it always dissipates mechanical energy.
  • Forgetting that power is the *rate* of work; dividing energy by time, not distance.
  • Using the wrong mass in momentum equations when objects stick together (use combined mass).
  • Neglecting to convert units (e.g., cm to m) before using formulas.
