📖 Complete Theory

Gravity is the universal attractive force that acts between any two masses. Sir Isaac Newton quantified this force in the 17th century, laying the groundwork for modern astrophysics and orbital mechanics. The law is expressed as

F = G · (m₁ m₂) / r²

where G = 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻², m₁ and m₂ are the interacting masses, and r is the distance between their centres. This simple relation explains why objects fall to Earth, why planets orbit the Sun, and why moons stay bound to planets.

Kepler’s Laws – From Observation to Theory

Johannes Kepler, using Tycho Brahe’s meticulous planetary data, formulated three empirical laws (1609‑1619). They are not merely historical footnotes; they are direct consequences of Newton’s gravitational law.

  • First Law (Law of Ellipses): Every planet describes an ellipse with the Sun at one focus. An ellipse is defined by its semi‑major axis a and eccentricity e. For Earth, e ≈ 0.0167, making the orbit almost circular.
  • Second Law (Law of Equal Areas): A line joining a planet and the Sun sweeps out equal areas in equal times. This implies that a planet moves faster when nearer to the Sun (perihelion) and slower at aphelion.
  • Third Law (Harmonic Law): The square of the orbital period T is proportional to the cube of the semi‑major axis a:

T² = (4π²/GM) a³

For any two bodies orbiting the same central mass, the ratio T₁²/a₁³ = T₂²/a₂³ holds true. This law is the backbone of satellite period calculations.

Artificial Satellites – Putting Theory into Practice

A satellite of mass m in a circular orbit of radius r around Earth experiences a centripetal force supplied by gravity:

m v²/r = G Mₑ m/r² → v = √(GMₑ/r)

The orbital period follows from v = 2πr/T:

T = 2π√(r³/GMₑ)

Key satellite categories used by the Indian defence forces:

  • Low‑Earth Orbit (LEO): 200–2,000 km; used for Earth‑observation, navigation, and early‑warning radars.
  • Medium‑Earth Orbit (MEO): ~20,000 km; GPS and GLONASS constellations.
  • Geostationary Orbit (GEO): 35,786 km; satellites appear stationary over the equator—ideal for communication and weather monitoring.

A practical example: the Indian Remote Sensing Satellite (IRS‑1A) at 720 km altitude completes an orbit in about 100 minutes, providing a ground‑track repeat cycle useful for reconnaissance.

Escape Velocity – Breaking Free from Gravity

Escape velocity is the minimum speed an object needs to leave a celestial body's gravitational field without further propulsion. Derivation from energy conservation gives:

½ m vₑ² = G M m/R → vₑ = √(2GM/R)

For Earth (M≈5.97 × 10²⁴ kg, R≈6.37 × 10⁶ m), vₑ ≈ 11.2 km s⁻¹. The Moon’s escape speed is only 2.38 km s⁻¹, a fact exploited by the Chandrayaan‑2 mission’s lander.

🧠 Memory Tricks & Mnemonics

  • “G‑Force = G‑R‑M‑S” – Remember the order: Gravitation, Radius, Mass, Speed (v = √GM/R).
  • “KEP‑ELER” – K = Keep equal areas, E = Ellipse, P = Period ∝ a³⁄² (Third law), L = Law of 2 (areas), ER = “Elliptical Right‑hand rule”.
  • “ESCAPE = 2 × G M / R” – The “2” reminds you that escape velocity is √(2 × …).
  • “GEOSTATIONARY = G‑E‑O‑S‑T” – “Guard Every Orbital Satellite To Keep Transmission”.
  • “MARS” for orbital speed: Mass of central body, Alitude (radius), R = √(GM/R), S = speed.
  • “B‑M‑A‑C” (Bombardier’s Missile Analogy) – Burn (heat), Mass, Altitude, Compute escape, useful for ballistic missile trajectories.

📝 PYQ Pattern Analysis

Past five years of CDS, AFCAT and NDA papers show gravitation questions appearing in 12‑15% of the physics section. The most recurring sub‑topics are Kepler’s laws (especially the third law), orbital speed/period calculations, and escape velocity. Questions are usually numerical with one‑step algebra, but occasionally a two‑step problem involving both Kepler’s law and Newton’s law is set to test conceptual integration.

AFCAT tends to favour quick‑calculation items – e.g., “What is the period of a satellite at 10,000 km altitude?” – while CDS includes a mix of conceptual statements (“If a planet’s orbital radius doubles, its period changes by…”) and multi‑step numerical problems. NDA often presents a diagram of an elliptical orbit and asks to locate periapsis/apapsis or compute eccentricity.

Common traps observed: mixing up radius with altitude, ignoring the Earth’s radius when using the orbital formula, and treating escape velocity as a “minimum speed after launch” rather than a theoretical value. Recent trends show an increase in questions linking gravitation to satellite communication (e.g., bandwidth, line‑of‑sight) – a hint to integrate physics with modern defence tech.

🎓 Examination Perspective

CDS: Usually asks derivations, conceptual statements, and multi‑step calculations (e.g., combining Kepler’s third law with gravitational force). Expect at least one question on satellite period and one on escape velocity.

AFCAT: Emphasises speed – 1‑mark MCQs with direct formulas. Typical items: “Find the orbital speed of a satellite at 500 km altitude” or “Escape speed from Mars?”. No heavy algebra.

NDA: Focuses on basics – definitions, law statements, and simple numerical problems. Frequently includes a diagram of an ellipse or a satellite orbit and asks for identification of focus/major axis.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • Using altitude instead of radius (r = Rₑ + altitude) in orbital formulas – leads to 30‑40% error.
  • Neglecting the factor 2 in escape velocity derivation – results in vₑ being off by √2.
  • Assuming Kepler’s third law applies to non‑central forces – it is valid only for inverse‑square law.
  • Mixing up periapsis/apapsis with perihelion/aphelion – keep the central body in mind.
  • Treating orbital period as dependent on satellite mass – it cancels out; period is mass‑independent.
  • For GEO, forgetting that the satellite must be above the equator to remain stationary – inclined GEOs drift.
  • Using the wrong value of G (e.g., 6.67 × 10⁻⁵) – always check units.
  • Confusing linear velocity with angular velocity – remember v = 2πr/T.
