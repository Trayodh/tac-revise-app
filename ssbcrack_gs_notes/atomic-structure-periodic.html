📖 Complete Theory

The atom, once thought to be indivisible, is now known to be a compact system of sub‑atomic particles. The positively charged proton and neutral neutron form the dense nucleus, while negatively charged electrons whirl around in defined energy regions called orbitals. The number of protons (atomic number, Z) uniquely identifies an element; the sum of protons and neutrons (mass number, A) gives its isotopic mass.

1. Quantum Mechanical Model

Erwin Schrödinger’s wave equation replaced the planetary model with a probability cloud. Each electron is described by a set of quantum numbers:

  • n (principal) – energy level (1,2,3…).
  • ℓ (azimuthal) – shape of orbital (s, p, d, f).
  • mℓ (magnetic) – orientation in space.
  • ms (spin) – +½ or –½.

These numbers obey three rules:

  1. Pauli Exclusion Principle: No two electrons in an atom can have identical sets of all four quantum numbers.
  2. Hund’s Rule: Electrons occupy degenerate orbitals singly before pairing.
  3. Aufbau Principle: Electrons fill lower‑energy orbitals first (1s → 2s → 2p → 3s …).

2. Electron Configuration & Notations

The arrangement of electrons is written as a series of superscripts, e.g., 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁴ for sulfur. The noble‑gas shorthand ([Ne] 3s² 3p⁴) shortens long strings and highlights valence electrons, which dictate chemical reactivity.

3. Periodic Table – Structure

Dmitri Mendeleev’s table organised elements by atomic mass; modern tables use atomic number. Key features:

  • Groups (1‑18) – elements share valence‑shell configuration.
  • Periods (1‑7) – each period adds a new electron shell.
  • Blocks – s‑block (Groups 1‑2 + He), p‑block (Groups 13‑18), d‑block (Transition metals), f‑block (Lanthanides & Actinides).

4. Periodic Trends

Understanding trends is vital for quick problem solving.

Property Trend Across a Period Trend Down a Group
Atomic Radius Decreases (Z_eff ↑) Increases (more shells)
Ionisation Energy (IE) Increases (Z_eff ↑) Decreases (shielding ↑)
Electron Affinity Generally becomes more negative Becomes less negative
Electronegativity Increases (Z_eff ↑) Decreases (shielding ↑)

5. Special Cases – Transition & Inner‑Transition Metals

Transition metals (d‑block) exhibit variable oxidation states because of comparable energies of (n‑1)d and ns electrons. This explains the colourful complexes used in camouflage and signalling. Lanthanides and actinides (f‑block) have high shielding, leading to low IE and large atomic radii despite high Z.

6. Practical Relevance to Defence Exams

Questions often test:

  • Identification of ground‑state electron configuration.
  • Predicting ion formation (cations from metals, anions from non‑metals).
  • Comparing two elements for bond type with fluorine.
  • Understanding why certain metals are chosen for aircraft alloys (e.g., high strength‑to‑weight ratio of Ti).

📚 Important Definitions

Term Definition
Atomic Number (Z)Number of protons in the nucleus; uniquely identifies an element.
Mass Number (A)Sum of protons and neutrons in the nucleus.
IsotopeAtoms of the same element (same Z) with different mass numbers.
Effective Nuclear Charge (Zeff)Net positive charge experienced by valence electrons; Z – σ (shielding constant).
Ionisation Energy (IE)Energy required to remove the outermost electron from a gaseous atom.
Electron Affinity (EA)Energy change when an electron is added to a neutral atom.
Electronegativity (EN)Ability of an atom to attract bonding electrons.
Hund’s RuleElectrons occupy degenerate orbitals singly before pairing.
Pauli Exclusion PrincipleNo two electrons can share the same set of four quantum numbers.
Aufbau PrincipleElectrons fill lower‑energy orbitals before higher‑energy ones.
Valence ElectronElectron(s) in the outermost shell; determine chemical behaviour.

🧠 Memory Tricks & Mnemonics

  • “S‑P‑D‑F – Soldiers Patrol Daily Frontline” – Remember the order of orbital filling (s, p, d, f).
  • “Li Na K – Light Infantry, Na – Night‑watch, K – K9 Unit” – Alkali metals are highly reactive, think of “quick‑action troops”.
  • “F‑Cl‑Br‑I – Frontline Combatants, Bigger the soldier, weaker the bite” – Halogen reactivity decreases down the group (F most reactive).
  • “EN = Z_eff – Shield” – Electronegativity equals effective nuclear charge minus shielding; picture a soldier (EN) breaking through enemy shield (σ).
  • “Ti‑Al‑Mg – Tactical Air‑lift Materials” – Remember the three light‑weight metals used in aircraft (Titanium, Aluminium, Magnesium).
  • “C‑N‑O – Command, Navigation, Operations” – The three most electronegative non‑metals, crucial for “battle‑field communications”.

⭐ Important Facts (Defence Exam Favourites)

  1. Atomic number of uranium is 92.
  2. First ionisation energy of helium is the highest among all elements.
  3. Fluorine has the highest electronegativity (3.98 on Pauling scale).
  4. Elements in the same group have similar chemical properties due to identical valence‑electron configuration.
  5. The d‑block contains 10 groups (3‑12) and houses transition metals.
  6. Lanthanides are also called “rare‑earth elements”.
  7. Effective nuclear charge increases across a period.
  8. Atomic radius decreases across a period.
  9. First ionisation energy decreases down a group.
  10. Electron configuration of Cu is [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹ (an exception).
  11. Gold (Au) has an electron configuration ending in 5d¹⁰ 6s¹.
  12. Alkali metals form +1 cations; alkaline earth metals form +2 cations.
  13. Transition metals often form coloured complexes due to d‑d transitions.
  14. Hydrogen is placed separately because it shows properties of both Group 1 and Group 17.
  15. Isotopes of carbon: ¹²C (stable), ¹³C (stable), ¹⁴C (radioactive).
  16. Atomic number of chlorine is 17; its valence shell contains 7 electrons.
  17. Calcium (Ca) belongs to the s‑block and has a configuration [Ar] 4s².
  18. First ionisation energy of Na is lower than that of Mg, despite higher atomic number.
  19. Electronegativity trend: F > O > N > C > B > Si.
  20. Atomic radius of Francium is the largest among naturally occurring elements.

🔢 Formula / Key Data Box

  • Z_eff = Z – σ (σ = shielding constant)
  • IE₁ ∝ Z_eff² / n² (First ionisation energy approximation)
  • EN (Pauling) = 0.102 × (Z_eff – σ) (rough estimate)
  • Atomic radius trend: ↓ across a period, ↑ down a group.
  • Electron configuration notation: [Noble gas] ns² np¹‑⁶ (nd/ (n‑1)d¹‑¹⁰) (nf/ (n‑2)f¹‑¹⁴).
  • Hund’s rule – maximum multiplicity.

📝 PYQ Pattern Analysis

Past Year Questions (PYQ) from CDS, AFCAT and NDA reveal a clear pattern: about 70% of chemistry questions stem from the “Atomic Structure & Periodic Table” chapter. The most recurring sub‑topics are electron configuration (especially anomalies like Cu, Cr), periodic trends (IE, EN, atomic radius) and the classification of elements (s‑block vs. d‑block). Questions are frequently presented in a “fill‑in‑the‑blank” format, asking for the correct configuration of a given element, or as a comparative statement (e.g., “Which element has a larger first IE, Mg or Al?”). Recent papers (2023‑2025) have introduced diagram‑based questions where candidates must locate an element on the Periodic Table based on its properties. Common traps include forgetting the (n‑1) rule for d‑orbitals and mixing up the order of filling between 4s and 3d.

🎓 Examination Perspective

CDS: Emphasises conceptual clarity. Expect 2–3 MCQs on electron configuration, 1–2 on periodic trends, and a short‑answer on the significance of Z_eff. Questions often link chemistry with physics (e.g., “Which element is used in the alloy of aircraft wings?”).

AFCAT: Focuses on speed. Usually 3 quick‑fire MCQs on trends and a single “match the column” type on block classification. No essay‑type questions.

NDA: Covers the entire syllabus but gives higher weightage to fundamentals. Expect 4–5 MCQs on configurations, a diagram‑based question on the Periodic Table, and a fill‑in‑the‑blank on ionisation energy values.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • Confusing the order of 4s and 3d filling – remember 4s fills before 3d.
  • Ignoring exceptions (Cu, Cr) and writing the textbook configuration.
  • Assuming atomic radius always increases down a group; lanthanides contract (lanthanide contraction).
  • Mixing up electronegativity trends with ionisation energy trends – they are related but not identical.
  • Using atomic number instead of mass number while writing isotopic notation.
  • Forgetting that helium belongs to the s‑block (1s²) despite being placed with noble gases.
  • Over‑relying on memorised numbers; better to understand the underlying Z_eff concept.
  • Neglecting the effect of shielding when comparing IE of elements in the same period.

  • Aluminium (Al) – primary material for transport aircraft and parachute rigs due to low density.

  • Nickel (Ni) – component of stainless steel used in naval hulls; corrosion resistance linked to d‑electron configuration.
  • Rare‑earth elements (e.g., Lanthanum, Cerium) – critical for high‑performance radar and communication systems.
