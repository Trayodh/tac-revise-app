⚡ Quick Revision

  • Acid = proton donor (Bronsted‑Lowry) or H⁺ source (Arrhenius).
  • Base = proton acceptor or OH⁻ source.
  • Salt = product of neutralization of acid and base.
  • pH = –log[H⁺]; pOH = –log[OH⁻]; pH + pOH = 14 (25 °C).
  • Strong acids (HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃) dissociate completely; weak acids (CH₃COOH, H₂CO₃) partially.
  • Strong bases (NaOH, KOH) dissociate completely; weak bases (NH₃) partially.
  • Indicators change color at specific pH ranges (e.g., phenolphthalein 8.2‑10).
  • Neutralization: Acid + Base → Salt + Water.
  • Buffer: mixture of weak acid & its conjugate base (or weak base & its conjugate acid) resists pH change.
  • Hydrolysis of salts determines solution’s acidity or basicity.

📖 Complete Theory

The world of acids, bases and salts is foundational for chemistry and for defence‑related applications such as corrosion control, propellant chemistry and biomedical monitoring. Below we build the topic from the ground up, using plain language and real‑life analogies.

1. What is an Acid?

In the Arrhenius sense, an acid releases H⁺ ions in water. In the Bronsted‑Lowry view, it is a proton donor. Strong acids (e.g., HCl, H₂SO₄) dissociate completely, giving a high concentration of H⁺, whereas weak acids (e.g., CH₃COOH) only partly ionise, leading to a relatively low [H⁺]. Think of a strong acid as a “full‑time soldier” who immediately gives up his weapon (proton) to the enemy (water), while a weak acid is a “reserve soldier” who hesitates.

2. What is a Base?

Bases are the opposite – they accept protons or release OH⁻ ions. Strong bases like NaOH dissociate fully, providing a flood of OH⁻; weak bases such as NH₃ accept protons only partially. Visualise a base as a “magnet” that pulls in the free protons released by acids.

3. Salts – The After‑math of Neutralization

When an acid reacts with a base, the products are a salt and water. The nature of the salt (acidic, basic, or neutral) depends on the strength of the parent acid and base. For example, NaCl (from strong acid HCl + strong base NaOH) is neutral, whereas NH₄Cl (from weak base NH₃ + strong acid HCl) is acidic because the cation hydrolyses.

4. The pH Scale – Measuring Acidity

pH = –log[H⁺] gives a convenient numeric scale from 0 (strongly acidic) to 14 (strongly basic). pOH = –log[OH⁻] and the relation pH + pOH = 14 (at 25 °C) link the two. A solution with [H⁺] = 1 × 10⁻⁴ M has pH = 4, indicating moderate acidity.

5. Indicators – The Colourful Spies

Indicators are weak acids or bases that change colour at specific pH ranges. Common ones include:

  • Litmus – red in acid, blue in base.
  • Phenolphthalein – colourless (pH < 8.2) → pink (pH > 10).
  • Methyl orange – red (pH < 3.1) → yellow (pH > 4.4).

In the field, these are used to quickly assess the acidity of water supplies, fuel, or ammunition cleaning solutions.

6. Neutralization & Titration

A neutralization reaction is a proton‑transfer process:

Acid + Base → Salt + H₂O

Titration uses a known concentration of an acid/base to determine the unknown concentration of its counterpart. The endpoint is identified by a colour change of an indicator.

7. Buffers – The Shock Absorbers

A buffer system contains a weak acid and its conjugate base (or vice‑versa). It resists pH change because added H⁺ or OH⁻ is neutralised by the buffer components. The classic example is the carbonic‑bicarbonate buffer in blood (H₂CO₃/HCO₃⁻), crucial for maintaining physiological pH.

8. Hydrolysis of Salts

When a salt dissolves, its ions may react with water (hydrolysis). The direction of hydrolysis decides the solution’s pH:

  • Salt of strong acid + strong base → neutral (e.g., NaCl).
  • Salt of strong acid + weak base → acidic (e.g., NH₄Cl).
  • Salt of weak acid + strong base → basic (e.g., NaCH₃COO).

9. Practical Relevance for Defence

*Corrosion control*: Acidic environments accelerate metal corrosion; neutralising agents (e.g., lime) protect naval hulls.
*Propellant chemistry*: Nitrocellulose and nitroglycerin formulations rely on controlled pH to avoid premature decomposition.
*Medical*: Blood‑gas analysis uses pH and bicarbonate values to assess a soldier’s physiological state.

10. Quick Calculation Tips

  • When given [H⁺], use pH = –log[H⁺] directly.
  • For weak acid/base, use Ka or Kb and ICE tables.
  • During titration, equivalence point moles of acid = moles of base.
  • Remember pH = 14 – pOH at 25 °C.

🧠 Memory Tricks & Mnemonics

  • “A BATTALION OF ACIDS” – Remember the strong acids: HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃, HBr, HI (think of a battalion marching in order of decreasing strength).
  • “BASE CAMP” – Strong bases are the “camp”: NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂ – all end with “OH”.
  • “PHEN O’PHAL” – Phenolphthalein turns pink when the “ph” (pH) is high – “ph” sounding like “ph” in “phalanx”.
  • “MATH = 14” – Quick recall that pH + pOH = 14 at 25 °C – think of a 14‑man squad.
  • “NEUTRAL = NUT” – Neutral salts are “nut‑like” – they don’t affect pH, just like a plain nut doesn’t change the flavor of a dish.
  • “BUFFER = BUNKER” – A buffer protects pH just like a bunker protects troops from shock.

  6. Basic salts are formed when a weak acid reacts with a strong base.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • Confusing Ka with Kb – remember Ka is for acids, Kb for bases.
  • Using pH = –log[OH⁻] instead of pOH.
  • Assuming all salts are neutral – check the strength of parent acid/base.
  • Ignoring the temperature dependence of the 14‑rule; at 50 °C, pH + pOH ≈ 13.6.
  • Choosing the wrong indicator range for a given titration – always match the equivalence point pH.
  • For weak acid/base calculations, forgetting to set up ICE tables.
  • Treating the endpoint as the same as the equivalence point – they differ when indicators are used.
  • Over‑looking the effect of hydrolysis on salt solutions.
