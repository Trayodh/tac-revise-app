📖 Complete Theory

Human nutrition is the science of how food provides the body with the necessary energy and building blocks for growth, repair and optimal performance. For defence personnel, the stakes are higher – a single nutrient deficit can impair night vision, reduce stamina, or compromise immunity during prolonged deployments.

1. Classification of Vitamins

Vitamins are grouped based on solubility:

  • Fat‑soluble (A, D, E, K) – stored in liver and adipose tissue; excess is stored, deficiency appears slowly.
  • Water‑soluble (B‑complex, C) – not stored in large amounts; excess is excreted in urine, so daily intake is essential.

3. Minerals – Macro vs. Trace

Minerals are inorganic elements required in varying amounts.

  • Major (macro) minerals: Calcium (Ca), Phosphorus (P), Potassium (K), Sodium (Na), Magnesium (Mg), Sulphur (S). They constitute >95% of the total mineral mass.
  • Trace minerals: Iron (Fe), Zinc (Zn), Copper (Cu), Iodine (I), Selenium (Se), Manganese (Mn). Though needed in minute quantities, they are vital for enzyme function and hormone synthesis.

For a soldier, calcium is the “armor” of bones, while iron is the “fuel” for hemoglobin, directly influencing aerobic capacity during marches.

4. Recommended Dietary Allowances (RDA) – Indian Context

The Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) provides age‑specific RDAs. For an adult male (18‑30 yr):

  • Energy: 2500 kcal/day
  • Protein: 60 g (≈ 20 % of energy)
  • Carbohydrate: 300–350 g (≈ 55 % of energy)
  • Fat: 70–80 g (≈ 30 % of energy)
  • Calcium: 1000 mg
  • Iron: 17 mg
  • Vitamin C: 40 mg
  • Vitamin D: 600 IU (15 µg)

These values form the backbone of a balanced diet – a mix of cereals, pulses, vegetables, fruits, dairy, and occasional meat/fish.

5. Deficiency & Toxicity – Clinical Correlation

Deficiency symptoms often mimic fatigue, making them easy to overlook in field conditions. For example:

  • Iron deficiency anemia → reduced VO₂ max, slower recovery.
  • Vitamin D deficiency → musculoskeletal pain, higher fracture risk during paratroop jumps.
  • Vitamin B1 deficiency → impaired carbohydrate utilisation → early exhaustion.

Conversely, hyper‑vitaminosis (excess) can be hazardous: Vitamin A toxicity may cause raised intracranial pressure, a serious concern for pilots.

6. Building a Balanced Diet – Practical Tips for Soldiers

  1. Follow the “Plate Method”: ½ plate vegetables & fruits, ¼ protein, ¼ complex carbs.
  2. Include one calcium‑rich item (milk, cheese, ragi) daily.
  3. Consume iron‑rich foods (red meat, beans) with Vitamin C to boost absorption.
  4. Prefer whole grains over refined flour to preserve B‑vitamins.
  5. Hydrate with electrolyte‑balanced water during heat‑exposure drills.
  6. Use fortified biscuits or energy bars during high‑intensity operations.

7. Nutrition in Extreme Environments

High altitude, desert, and maritime conditions alter nutrient needs. At altitude, Vitamin D synthesis drops due to reduced UV‑B; supplementing 800–1000 IU/day is advised. In deserts, sodium loss via sweat is high – a 1 L loss may contain 2 g Na⁺; replace with oral rehydration salts.

  • “B‑C‑D‑E‑F‑G” – “Boys Can Do Excellent Field Games”: Helps recall water‑soluble B‑complex (B1‑B12) and Vitamin C.
  • “IRON‑ZINC‑IODINE‑SE” – “I ROnly Z INvest C E”: Quick cue for trace minerals.
  • “CAL‑PO4‑MAG‑SOD‑K” – “Call All Soldiers, Keep Moving”: Major minerals in the order they appear in many textbooks.

  • “Beri‑Beri = B‑R‑I (Break‑Rapid‑In) Energy”: Shows that lack of B1 breaks rapid carbohydrate metabolism.

⭐ Important Facts (Defence Exam Favourites)

  1. Vitamin A is essential for rhodopsin synthesis in the retina.
  2. Deficiency of Vitamin B1 leads to beriberi, a common disease among troops on polished rice diets.
  3. Vitamin C deficiency (scurvy) was a major cause of deaths in 19th‑century naval expeditions.
  4. Vitamin D is synthesized when skin is exposed to UV‑B (290‑315 nm).
  5. Calcium absorption is maximized by Vitamin D and inhibited by excess phosphates.
  6. Iron deficiency reduces oxygen‑carrying capacity, decreasing VO₂ max by up to 15 %.
  7. High altitude reduces dietary iron absorption; supplementation is advised above 3000 m.
  8. Excess Vitamin A can cause teratogenic effects in pregnant women.
  9. Vitamin E protects polyunsaturated fatty acids in cell membranes from peroxidation.
  10. Vitamin K is required for γ‑carboxylation of clotting factors II, VII, IX, X.
  11. Magnesium is a co‑factor for over 300 enzymatic reactions, including ATP synthesis.
  12. Sodium‑potassium pump maintains nerve impulse; loss of Na⁺ leads to muscle cramps.
  13. Iodine deficiency causes goitre and cretinism; iodised salt prevents it.
  14. Selenium is part of glutathione peroxidase, defending cells from oxidative stress.
  15. RDA of protein for a soldier in training is 1.2–1.5 g/kg body weight.
  16. Balanced diet plate: ½ veg‑fruit, ¼ protein, ¼ complex carbs.
  17. Pulse‑based diets provide both protein and iron, improving endurance.
  18. Hyper‑vitaminosis D leads to hypercalcaemia, causing renal calculi.
  19. Vitamin C enhances non‑heme iron absorption by reducing Fe³⁺ to Fe²⁺.
  20. During desert operations, a soldier can lose up to 2 L of sweat per hour, containing ~2 g Na⁺.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • Confusing Vitamin D (hormone) with Vitamin C (antioxidant) – remember “D = D‑bone, C = Collagen”.
  • Assuming iron is stored in the liver like fat‑soluble vitamins – iron is stored mainly in bone marrow and spleen.
  • Mix‑up of RDA units (mg vs. µg) – Vitamin D is expressed in IU/µg; calcium in mg.
  • Ignoring the need for Vitamin C to enhance non‑heme iron absorption.
  • Over‑reliance on supplements without considering food matrix interactions.
  • Believing that all minerals are equally lost in sweat – sodium and potassium are predominant.
  • Treating “balanced diet” as “low‑fat” only; protein and micronutrient adequacy are equally vital.
  • Neglecting the effect of high‑altitude hypoxia on Vitamin D synthesis.
