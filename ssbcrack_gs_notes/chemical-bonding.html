⚡ Quick Revision

  • Ionic bonds: transfer of electrons, high lattice energy, solid crystals.
  • Covalent bonds: sharing of electrons, molecular or network solids.
  • Metallic bonds: delocalised electron sea, ductile, conductive.
  • Electronegativity difference > 1.7 → ionic character.
  • ΔEN 0.4‑1.7 → polar covalent; <0.4 → non‑polar covalent.
  • Bond polarity ↔ dipole moment; higher polarity = higher boiling point.
  • Lewis structures use dots for valence electrons; octet rule for main‑group elements.
  • Resonance stabilises molecules like O₃, NO₃⁻.
  • Hybridisation (sp, sp², sp³) explains bond angles.
  • Born‑Haber cycle links sublimation, ionisation, electron affinity, lattice energy.
  • Metallic radius = half the distance between nuclei in the lattice.
  • Alloys: mixture of metals, often improve strength & corrosion resistance.
  • Conductivity in metals due to free electrons; decreases with temperature rise.
  • Melting point trend: ionic > metallic > covalent (molecular).
  • Electrolytes: ionic compounds in water → conduct electricity.

📖 Complete Theory

The concept of chemical bonding is the cornerstone of chemistry. In defence‑related examinations, a clear grasp of how atoms stick together helps you answer questions on material strength, explosives, and even electronic warfare. This chapter builds from the simplest electron‑transfer picture to the collective behaviour of electrons in a metal lattice.

1. Ionic Bonding

When the electronegativity difference (ΔEN) between two atoms exceeds ~1.7, the more electronegative atom pulls an electron completely from the other. The donor becomes a cation (+) and the acceptor an anion (–). The resulting electrostatic attraction is the ionic bond. Classic examples are NaCl, MgO and KBr. Ionic solids form a regular three‑dimensional arrangement called a crystal lattice. The energy required to separate one mole of an ionic solid into gaseous ions is the lattice energy (U), a key indicator of stability.

Key properties:

  • High melting & boiling points (U is large).
  • Hard and brittle – planes of ions can slip, causing fracture.
  • Conduct electricity only when molten or dissolved (electrolytes).
  • Soluble in polar solvents (water) due to ion–dipole interactions.

2. Covalent Bonding

If ΔEN lies between 0.4 and 1.7, atoms share electrons rather than transfer them. This sharing creates a covalent bond. The simplest representation is the Lewis dot structure, where each dot stands for a valence electron. Atoms strive to achieve an octet (or duet for H and He). Covalent bonds can be:

  • Non‑polar – equal sharing (e.g., H₂, Cl₂).
  • Polar – unequal sharing, giving rise to a dipole (e.g., H₂O, NH₃).

Molecules may exhibit resonance when more than one Lewis structure can describe the electron distribution (e.g., O₃, NO₃⁻). Resonance stabilises the molecule by delocalising electrons over several atoms.

Hybridisation explains bond angles: sp³ (tetrahedral, 109.5°), sp² (trigonal planar, 120°), sp (linear, 180°). Covalent solids like diamond (C) and SiO₂ have a 3‑D network of strong bonds, giving them very high melting points.

3. Metallic Bonding

Metals possess a sea of delocalised electrons that are not bound to any particular nucleus. The positively charged ion cores are embedded in this electron cloud, creating an attractive force called the metallic bond. This model explains why metals are:

  • Good conductors of heat and electricity (free electrons transport energy).
  • Highly malleable and ductile (layers can slide without breaking the bond).
  • Shiny (electrons reflect incident light).

Alloys (e.g., steel, brass) are engineered by mixing different metals to tailor strength, corrosion resistance, and magnetic properties. The strength of a metallic bond depends on the number of delocalised electrons per atom and the size of the ion cores.

📚 Important Definitions

Term Definition
Ionic Bond Electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions formed by complete electron transfer.
Lattice Energy (U) Energy released when one mole of gaseous ions forms an ionic solid.
Covalent Bond Bond formed by sharing of electron pairs between atoms.
Polar Covalent Unequal sharing of electrons leading to partial charges.
Resonance Delocalisation of electrons over two or more Lewis structures.
Hybridisation Mixing of atomic orbitals to form new hybrid orbitals (sp, sp², sp³).
Metallic Bond Attraction between a lattice of positive ions and a sea of delocalised electrons.
Electronegativity (EN) A measure of an atom’s ability to attract electrons in a bond.
Born‑Haber Cycle Thermodynamic cycle used to calculate lattice energy of an ionic solid.
Alloy Mixture of two or more metals (or a metal with non‑metal) designed for specific properties.

🧠 Memory Tricks & Mnemonics

  • “Ionic = IONic, IONic = IONic”: Remember that a full ION moves from metal to non‑metal – the word “IONic” already contains the word “ION”.
  • “COVALENT = CO-VA‑LENT, CO = Company, VA = Value, LENT = Lent (shared)”: Imagine two companies sharing the same “lent” (loan) – they share resources (electrons).
  • “METAL = ME + TALL”: A tall soldier (ME) can carry a heavy “load” (electron sea) without breaking – visualise a tall soldier marching with a sack of electrons.
  • “R‑E‑S‑O‑N‑A‑N‑C‑E” for Resonance: “R-Enemy‑S‑Only‑N‑A‑C‑E” – Even enemies (different structures) can share a common “ace” (electron) for stability.
  • “BORN‑HABER = BORN to HABIT”: Think of a newborn (BORN) habitually arranging its toys (ions) – the cycle of steps (sublimation, ionisation, etc.) is a habit.

⭐ Important Facts (Defence Exam Favourites)

  1. ΔEN > 1.7 generally leads to an ionic bond.
  2. Lattice energy is always exothermic for stable ionic solids.
  3. NaCl melts at 801 °C, a typical high melting point for ionic compounds.
  4. Diamond’s hardness is due to a 3‑D covalent network of sp³ bonds.
  5. Graphite conducts electricity because of delocalised π‑electrons (a metallic‑like feature).
  6. Aluminium alloys (e.g., Duralumin) are used in aircraft for high strength‑to‑weight ratio.
  7. Electronegativity of fluorine (3.98) is the highest among all elements.
  8. MgO has a lattice energy of ≈ 3790 kJ mol⁻¹, one of the highest for binary oxides.
  9. Transition metals often form metallic bonds with partially filled d‑orbitals, giving rise to colored compounds.
  10. Hydrogen bonding is a special case of strong polar covalent interaction, crucial in water’s high boiling point.
  11. Metals become superconductors below a critical temperature (e.g., Nb at 9.2 K).
  12. Alloys like stainless steel contain Cr to improve corrosion resistance – vital for naval vessels.
  13. Born‑Haber cycle can be used to estimate the enthalpy of formation of any ionic compound.
  14. In covalent molecules, bond dipole moments add vectorially to give a net dipole moment.
  15. Mercury is the only metal liquid at room temperature, owing to relativistic effects on its 6s electrons.
  16. Silicon, a semiconductor, forms covalent bonds in a diamond‑like lattice.
  17. Metallic bonding explains why the Earth’s core is liquid iron‑nickel alloy despite high pressure.
  18. Ammonia (NH₃) is a polar covalent molecule with a trigonal pyramidal shape (sp³).
  19. Bond energy of H–H is 436 kJ mol⁻¹; O=O double bond is 498 kJ mol⁻¹.
  20. Electrolytic dissociation of NaCl in water produces Na⁺ and Cl⁻, making the solution conductive.

🔢 Formula / Key Data Box

1. Lattice Energy (U):  U = Σ (sublimation + ionisation + electron affinity + bond dissociation) – ΔH_f°

2. Electronegativity Difference (ΔEN): ΔEN = | EN_A – EN_B |

3. Bond Energy (D): D = Σ (bond dissociation energies of products) – Σ (bond dissociation energies of reactants)

4. Percent Ionic Character (%IC): %IC = (1 – e^(–0.25·(ΔEN)²))·100

5. Conductivity (σ) for metals: σ = n·e·μ (n = electron concentration, e = charge, μ = mobility)

6. Boiling point trend: Ionic > Network Covalent > Molecular Covalent > Metallic (depends on electron sea).

📝 PYQ Pattern Analysis

Past Year Questions (PYQs) from CDS, AFCAT and NDA consistently allocate 10‑15% of the chemistry section to bonding. The most frequent sub‑topic is “ionic compounds – lattice energy and solubility”, followed by “covalent structures – resonance and hybridisation”. Questions often appear in the form of numerical problems (Born‑Haber cycle) or conceptual MCQs (identifying bond type from ΔEN). Recent trends show a rise in “application‑oriented” questions where candidates must link bond type to material properties such as hardness of armour plates or conductivity of radar cables. Common traps involve confusing metallic conductivity with covalent (graphite) and overlooking the role of d‑orbitals in transition‑metal bonding.

🎓 Examination Perspective

CDS: Emphasises the link between bond type and material strength (e.g., why steel is preferred for tank hulls). Expect numerical problems on lattice energy and qualitative questions on conductivity.

AFCAT: Focuses on rapid identification – ΔEN values, electronegativity trends, and simple stoichiometric calculations. Frequently asks about alloys used in aircraft.

NDA: Prefers conceptual clarity: Lewis structures, resonance, and hybridisation. Often includes diagram‑based questions (e.g., drawing the structure of CO₂).

⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • Assuming all metals are conductive – forgetting that some alloys (e.g., stainless steel) have lower conductivity.
  • Mixing up lattice energy with enthalpy of formation; lattice energy is always exothermic for stable salts.
  • Using ΔEN > 2.0 as the sole rule for ionic bonds – some compounds (e.g., BeCl₂) are covalent despite high ΔEN.
  • Neglecting resonance in molecules like NO₃⁻, leading to incorrect bond order.
  • Confusing metallic bond with covalent network (diamond) – both are strong, but only metals have free electrons.
  • Forgetting that polar covalent molecules have dipole moments that affect boiling point.
  • Applying the octet rule to transition metals without considering d‑electron participation.
  • Over‑generalising that all ionic compounds are soluble in water – LiF is sparingly soluble.
