📖 Complete Theory

The human nervous system is a highly organised network that controls every activity of the body. It is divided into two major parts – the central nervous system (CNS) comprising the brain and spinal cord, and the peripheral nervous system (PNS) which includes all nerves outside the CNS. This chapter focuses on the CNS and the reflex mechanism, both of which are high‑yield topics for defence examinations.

1. Brain – The Command Centre

The brain is encased in the skull and protected by three meninges (dura, arachnoid, pia). It is divided into three major parts:

  • Cerebrum – the largest part, responsible for conscious thought, memory, language and voluntary movement. It has two hemispheres, each divided into four lobes:
    • Frontal lobe: motor cortex, decision‑making, personality.
    • Parietal lobe: sensory cortex, spatial orientation.
    • Temporal lobe: auditory cortex, memory (hippocampus).
    • Occipital lobe: visual processing.
  • Cerebellum – located posterior to the brain‑stem; coordinates balance, posture and fine muscle movements. It receives proprioceptive input from the spinal cord and vestibular system.
  • Brain‑stem – includes the medulla oblongata, pons and midbrain. It regulates vital autonomic functions such as respiration, heart rate, and blood pressure. The medulla houses the cardiovascular centre and respiratory centre.

2. Spinal Cord – The Highway of Signals

The spinal cord extends from the foramen magnum to the L1‑L2 vertebral level. It is protected by the vertebral column and surrounded by CSF. Its cross‑section shows a butterfly‑shaped grey matter (nerve cell bodies) surrounded by white matter (myelinated axons).

It is divided into 31 segments:

Region Segments Key Features
Cervical C1‑C8 Braches to upper limbs; C5‑C6 control diaphragm (phrenic nerve).
Thoracic T1‑T12 Innervates intercostal muscles; part of sympathetic chain.
Lumbar L1‑L5 Supplies lower limbs; major motor & sensory pathways.
Sacral S1‑S5 Controls pelvic organs; important for reflexes like anal wink.
Coccygeal Co1 Small, vestigial segment.

3. Reflex Action – The Body’s Quick‑Response System

A reflex is an involuntary, rapid response to a stimulus that does not involve the brain. The classic example is the stretch (myotatic) reflex – the knee‑jerk test.

The basic components of a reflex arc are:

  • Receptor – sensory organ that detects the stimulus (e.g., muscle spindle).
  • Sensory neuron – carries impulse to the spinal cord.
  • Integration centre – either a single synapse (monosynaptic) or interneurons (polysynaptic) within the spinal grey matter.
  • Motor neuron – conducts impulse to the effector.
  • Effector – muscle or gland that produces the response.

Monosynaptic reflexes are faster because they involve only one synapse (e.g., patellar reflex). Polysynaptic reflexes, such as the withdrawal reflex, incorporate interneurons and allow for more complex responses.

4. Clinical Correlates for Defence Personnel

Understanding the nervous system aids in rapid assessment on the battlefield:

  • GCS (Glasgow Coma Scale) – evaluates consciousness after head injury.
  • Spinal shock vs neurogenic shock – differentiate for appropriate resuscitation.
  • Recognition of upper vs lower motor neuron lesions guides physiotherapy and prognosis.
  • Reflex testing (e.g., plantar reflex) helps localise spinal cord damage.
  • Knowledge of the blood‑brain barrier influences drug choice in neuro‑trauma.

5. Summary Analogy

Think of the brain as a headquarters (strategic command), the spinal cord as a high‑speed railway (logistics), and reflexes as automated defence turrets that fire without waiting for orders from HQ – essential for a soldier’s quick reaction.

📚 Important Definitions

Term Definition
Neuron Basic functional unit of the nervous system; a cell that transmits electrical impulses.
Grey Matter Region in CNS containing neuronal cell bodies, dendrites, and unmyelinated axons.
White Matter Myelinated axon tracts that form the communication highways of the CNS.
Meninges Three protective membranes – dura mater, arachnoid mater, pia mater – surrounding brain and spinal cord.
Reflex Arc Neural pathway that mediates a reflex; includes receptor, sensory neuron, integration centre, motor neuron, effector.
Monosynaptic Reflex A reflex involving a single synapse between sensory and motor neuron (e.g., knee‑jerk).
Polysynaptic Reflex A reflex that includes one or more interneurons between sensory and motor neurons.
Blood‑Brain Barrier (BBB) Selective permeability barrier that restricts passage of substances from blood to brain tissue.
Upper Motor Neuron Neurons that originate in the brain and descend to the spinal cord; lesions cause spasticity.
Lower Motor Neuron Neurons that exit the spinal cord to innervate muscles; lesions cause flaccid paralysis.
Acetylcholine (ACh) Primary excitatory neurotransmitter at the neuromuscular junction.
Glial Cells Supportive cells in CNS (e.g., astrocytes, oligodendrocytes) that maintain homeostasis.

⭐ Important Facts (Defence Exam Favourites)

  1. The cerebrum accounts for about 85% of the brain’s total weight.
  2. Motor cortex is located in the precentral gyrus of the frontal lobe.
  3. The medulla oblongata controls heart rate, blood pressure and respiration.
  4. Each cervical spinal segment gives rise to a pair of spinal nerves; C5‑C6 also supply the diaphragm.
  5. Myelination of axons in the CNS is performed by oligodendrocytes.
  6. The blood‑brain barrier restricts molecules larger than 500 Da.
  7. Acetylcholine is the only neurotransmitter at the neuromuscular junction.
  8. Monosynaptic reflexes have the shortest latency (~30 ms).
  9. Polysynaptic reflexes involve interneurons, allowing for withdrawal from painful stimuli.
  10. Upper motor neuron lesions cause hyperreflexia, spasticity and Babinski sign.
  11. Lower motor neuron lesions cause muscle atrophy, fasciculations and flaccid paralysis.
  12. CSF is produced mainly by the choroid plexus in the lateral ventricles.
  13. Glial cells outnumber neurons by a ratio of about 10:1.
  14. The cerebellum contains more neurons than the rest of the brain combined.
  15. Spinal shock resolves in 24‑48 hours, after which reflexes return.
  16. GCS score ≤ 8 indicates severe brain injury.
  17. The posterior column‑medial lemniscal pathway carries fine touch and proprioception.
  18. Spinothalamic tract transmits pain and temperature sensations.
  19. Neurogenic shock is characterised by bradycardia and hypotension.
  20. Rigor mortis is a post‑mortem stiffening caused by depletion of ATP in muscle fibers.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • Confusing the functions of the frontal and parietal lobes – remember: frontal = motor, personality; parietal = sensory.
  • Assuming the cerebellum controls consciousness – it only fine‑tunes movement.
  • Mixing up upper and lower motor neuron signs – check for spasticity vs flaccidity.
  • Neglecting the role of interneurons in polysynaptic reflexes – they are essential for withdrawal responses.
  • Over‑looking the spinal cord’s segmental organisation – each segment corresponds to a pair of spinal nerves.
  • Forgetting that the medulla, not the pons, houses the cardiac and respiratory centres.
  • Believing that reflexes are always “fast”; some polysynaptic reflexes can be relatively slow.
  • Misinterpreting GCS – a score of 15 is normal; ≤ 8 indicates severe brain injury.
