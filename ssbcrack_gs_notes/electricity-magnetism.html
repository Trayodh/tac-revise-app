📖 Complete Theory

Ohm’s Law is the cornerstone of electric circuit analysis. It states that the current (I) flowing through a conductor between two points is directly proportional to the voltage (V) across the two points and inversely proportional to the resistance (R) of the conductor: V = I R. This simple relation holds true for ohmic materials where temperature remains constant. In defence labs, resistive heating (I²R) is a key factor in designing power‑distribution units for field equipment.

Series and Parallel Circuits

In a series circuit, components share a single path for current; therefore, the same current flows through each component while the total voltage is the sum of individual voltage drops. The total resistance adds up: R_total = R₁ + R₂ + … + R_n. Think of soldiers marching in a single file—if one soldier (resistor) slows down, the whole column slows.

Conversely, a parallel circuit offers multiple paths. The voltage across each branch is identical, while the currents divide according to branch resistance. The reciprocal of total resistance equals the sum of reciprocals: 1/R_total = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + … + 1/R_n. Imagine a platoon spreading out on different routes; the overall “traffic” (current) is the sum of each route’s flow.

Kirchhoff’s Laws

KVL (Kirchhoff’s Voltage Law) is a direct extension of energy conservation: the algebraic sum of all voltages around any closed loop equals zero. KCL (Kirchhoff’s Current Law) follows charge conservation: the sum of currents entering a node equals the sum leaving it. These laws become indispensable for solving complex “mixed” circuits where series‑parallel simplification is not straightforward.

Alternating Current (AC) Fundamentals

Unlike DC, AC voltage varies sinusoidally. The peak value (Vₚ) is √2 times the RMS (root‑mean‑square) value, which represents the equivalent DC voltage delivering the same power. RMS power formula: P_avg = V_rms I_rms. In defence applications, RMS values are used to rate generators, radar transmitters, and communication equipment.

Electromagnetic Induction

When a magnetic flux (Φ) through a coil changes with time, an emf is induced: ε = -dΦ/dt (Faraday’s Law). The negative sign embodies Lenz’s Law, stating that the induced emf creates a magnetic field opposing the original change—much like a soldier pushing back against an advancing enemy.

The magnitude of induced emf in a coil of N turns is ε = -N (dΦ/dt). For a solenoid, Φ = B A, where B is flux density and A is cross‑sectional area. Self‑inductance (L) quantifies a coil’s ability to induce emf in itself: L = NΦ/I. Energy stored in an inductor is ½ L I², a principle exploited in pulse‑power systems for rail‑guns.

RL, RC and RLC Circuits

In an RL circuit, the time constant τ = L/R dictates how fast current reaches its steady state. The current grows as I(t) = I_max(1 - e^{-t/τ}). In RC circuits, τ = RC, governing voltage across a capacitor: V(t) = V_max e^{-t/τ}. RLC circuits combine resistance, inductance, and capacitance, producing damped oscillations. The natural frequency ω₀ = 1/√(LC) and damping factor ζ = R/(2√(L/C)) determine whether the system is under‑damped (used in sonar) or critically damped (desired for stable communications).

Transformers and Power Transmission

A transformer uses two coupled coils to step voltage up or down while conserving power (neglecting losses). Voltage ratio equals turns ratio: V₁/V₂ = N₁/N₂. Power transmission over long distances relies on high‑voltage, low‑current transmission to minimise I²R losses—a principle behind the high‑voltage lines feeding forward bases.

Practical Defence Examples

  • Radar transmitters use high‑frequency AC in resonant cavities; the induced emf in the antenna coil follows Faraday’s law.
  • Rail‑gun pulsed power stores energy in massive inductors (½ L I²) and releases it in microseconds.
  • Electronic warfare (EW) suites rely on precise impedance matching (using series‑parallel networks) to avoid signal reflections.

🧠 Memory Tricks & Mnemonics

  • “OHM‑SIR” – Remember Ohm’s Law (V = I R) by picturing a Sir (officer) shouting “V is I R!” on the battlefield.
  • “KVL = Keep Very Light” – Think of a light‑weight soldier marching in a loop; the total “energy” he carries is zero.
  • “Lenz = Lend‑z” – “Lend” back the change; the induced current “lends” opposition to the original motion.
  • “RMS = ‘Really Massive Soldier’” – The RMS value is the “real” power a soldier can deliver, just like a massive soldier’s strength.
  • “FARE‑DAYS” for Faraday – F (Flux) A (Area) R (Rate) E (∂) D (Δ) A (Amplitude) Y (Yield) S (Sign) – a quick cue for ε = -dΦ/dt.
  • “TRAINER” for Transformers – T (Turns ratio) R (Ratio of voltage) A (Ampere adjustment) I (Induced) N (No loss ideal) E (Efficiency) R (Reverse polarity).

⭐ Important Facts (Defence Exam Favourites)

  1. Resistance of a copper wire doubles when temperature rises by 30 °C.
  2. Standard NATO communication cables use a 50 Ω characteristic impedance.
  3. Faraday’s original experiment used a copper ring and a magnet.
  4. The RMS value of a 120 V, 60 Hz sinusoidal supply is 120 V (by definition).
  5. Self‑inductance of a solenoid: L = μ₀ N² A / l.
  6. In a series RL circuit, the current lags the voltage by tan⁻¹(ωL/R).
  7. Transformers are 95‑99 % efficient in modern power stations.
  8. Skin depth δ = √(2ρ/ωμ); at 60 Hz, copper’s skin depth ≈ 8.5 mm.
  9. Mutual inductance of two coaxial coils: M = k√(L₁L₂), where k ≤ 1.
  10. Time constant τ = L/R for an RL circuit determines 63 % rise time.
  11. Capacitive reactance X_C = 1/(ωC); at 50 Hz, a 10 µF capacitor has X_C ≈ 318 Ω.
  12. Electrical power in a resistor can also be expressed as P = I²R.
  13. Ohm’s law fails for non‑ohmic devices like diodes and transistors.
  14. In a parallel circuit, total resistance is always less than the smallest individual resistance.
  15. Induced emf in a moving conductor: ε = B l v (when cutting magnetic lines).
  16. The Earth’s magnetic field (~0.5 Gauss) can induce measurable emf in long power lines during storms.
  17. Rail‑gun pulse generators often use megahenry inductors.
  18. Kirchhoff’s laws are valid only for lumped‑parameter circuits (size ≪ wavelength).
  19. Maximum power transfer occurs when load resistance equals source internal resistance.
  20. In AC circuits, apparent power S = V_rms I_rms (VA), while real power P = V_rms I_rms cos ϕ.

📝 PYQ Pattern Analysis

Past‑year questions from CDS, AFCAT and NDA reveal a clear bias toward conceptual understanding rather than rote calculation. Approximately 35 % of the questions target Ohm’s law and simple series/parallel circuits, often framed as “find the current through a resistor” with three‑digit numbers. Another 25 % focus on electromagnetic induction, especially Faraday’s law and Lenz’s rule, usually presented as a moving conductor in a magnetic field. RL and RC time‑constant problems make up 15 % and are usually “what is the current after t seconds?” type. The remaining 25 % are mixed‑type – transformer ratios, RMS‑power conversions, and a few conceptual KCL/KVL statements.

Recent trends (2022‑2024) show an increase in questions linking circuits to defence equipment – e.g., “a radar transmitter uses a 2 µF capacitor; calculate the resonant frequency”. The traps often involve sign errors in Lenz’s law, forgetting the negative sign, or mixing RMS with peak values. Paying attention to units (henry, farad, ohm) and using quick approximations (π≈3) helps in time‑pressured settings.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • Ignoring the negative sign in Faraday’s law – leads to wrong direction of induced emf.
  • Mixing RMS and peak values – especially in power calculations; always convert before using P = V I.

    • Incorrectly adding resistances in parallel – remember to use reciprocal sum.

  • Assuming current is the same in parallel branches – only voltage is common.
  • Using KVL on a circuit whose dimensions are comparable to wavelength – lumped‑parameter assumption fails.
  • Overlooking the effect of internal resistance of a source when applying maximum power transfer theorem.
