## 1. Introduction
The British economic policy in India evolved through three distinct stages, transforming India from a major exporter of manufactured goods into a classic colonial economy: an exporter of raw materials and a captive market for British manufactured goods.

### The Three Stages of British Capitalism in India (as analyzed by R.P. Dutt):
1.  **Phase of Mercantilism (1757 – 1813):** The [[East India Company]] used its political [[power]] to establish a monopoly over trade and dictate terms to Indian artisans. Plundered wealth from Bengal was used to finance the Industrial Revolution in England.
2.  **Phase of Free Trade (1813 – 1858):** India was aggressively converted into a market for British industrial goods (especially cheap machine-made cotton textiles) and a supplier of raw materials (raw cotton, jute, indigo). This led to the massive de-industrialization of India.
3.  **Phase of Financial Imperialism (1858 onwards):** British capital was invested heavily in India in areas like railways, banking, tea/coffee plantations, and shipping to extract higher profits. The railways were built primarily to penetrate the Indian market with British goods and export raw materials easily, rather than to benefit the Indian economy.

## 2. Land Revenue Systems
To maximize their income, the British introduced new land revenue systems that fundamentally altered agrarian relations, creating a new class of landlords and reducing cultivators to impoverished tenants.

### A. Permanent Settlement (Zamindari System)
*   **Introduced by:** Lord Cornwallis in 1793.
*   **Areas:** Bengal, Bihar, Orissa, and parts of northern Madras (covered ~19% of British India).
*   **Features:**
    *   **Zamindars** (previously mere revenue collectors) were recognized as the absolute owners of the land.
    *   The revenue demand was fixed in perpetuity. The state's share was fixed at 10/11th of the rent, while the Zamindar kept 1/11th.
    *   **Sunset Clause:** If a Zamindar failed to pay the revenue by sunset on a specified date, his estate was confiscated and auctioned off.
*   **Impact:** Created a loyal class of Zamindars for the British but led to severe oppression and rack-renting of the peasants, giving rise to absentee landlordism.

### B. Ryotwari System
*   **Introduced by:** Thomas Munro and Captain Alexander Read (around 1820).
*   **Areas:** Madras, Bombay, parts of Assam and Coorg (covered ~51% of British India).
*   **Features:**
    *   The settlement was made directly with the cultivator (**Ryot**), eliminating intermediaries.
    *   The Ryot was recognized as the owner of the land as long as they paid the revenue.
    *   Revenue rates were exceptionally high (up to 50% on dry lands) and were revised periodically (usually every 20-30 years).
*   **Impact:** Instead of private Zamindars, the State itself became a giant Zamindar. Peasants frequently lost their lands to moneylenders due to the high revenue demands.

### C. Mahalwari System
*   **Introduced by:** Holt Mackenzie (1822) and modified under William Bentinck (1833).
*   **Areas:** North-Western Provinces (UP), Central Provinces, parts of Punjab (covered ~30% of British India).
*   **Features:**
    *   The settlement was made with the entire village community (**Mahal**) jointly.
    *   Village headmen (Lambardars) were responsible for collecting the revenue and paying it to the government.
    *   The revenue was periodically revised.

## 3. The Drain of Wealth Theory
The nationalist critique of the colonial economy was pioneered by **Dadabhai Naoroji** (The Grand Old Man of India) in his seminal book, **Poverty and Un-British Rule in India**.

*   **Concept:** He argued that a massive portion of India's national wealth was being unilaterally transferred to Britain with no corresponding economic, commercial, or material return.
*   **Constituents of the Drain:**
    *   **Home Charges:** This was the biggest component. It included the salaries and pensions of British civil and military officials in India, interest on public debt raised in England, and dividends to EIC shareholders.
    *   Remittances of savings by British officials.
    *   Profits of British trading agencies and companies.
*   **Other Proponents:** R.C. Dutt (author of *The Economic History of India*), M.G. Ranade, and G.V. Joshi.
*   **Impact:** The continuous drain systematically impoverished India, crippled capital formation, and caused frequent, devastating famines (like the Bengal Famine of 1770 and the Famine of 1899).

## 4. Commercialization of Agriculture
*   **Definition:** The shift from cultivating [[crops]] for self-consumption (food grains) to cultivating cash [[crops]] for the market (especially for export to European factories).
*   **Major Cash [[Crops]]:** Cotton, Jute, Indigo, Sugarcane, Tea, Coffee, Opium.
*   **Impact:**
    *   While it integrated the Indian village into the global market, it ruined the food security of the Indian peasant. 
    *   The infamous **Tinkathia system** in Champaran forced peasants to grow Indigo on 3/20th of their land under highly exploitative terms, leading to the Champaran Satyagraha.
    *   It increased the reliance of the peasant on the moneylender for cash to pay high revenues and buy food, trapping them in a vicious cycle of debt.
