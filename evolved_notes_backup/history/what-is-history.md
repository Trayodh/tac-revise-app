# What is History?

## 1. Defining History and Its Scope
History is the systematic study of the human past, reconstructed through the careful analysis of surviving records, artifacts, and oral traditions. Rather than a mere chronological catalog of events, true historical study involves interpreting the complex interplay of social, economic, political, and cultural forces that shape civilizations. For competitive defense examinations, understanding historiography—the study of how history is written—is crucial, as it builds analytical reasoning and chronological awareness. History provides the context for geopolitical [[evolution]], human geography, and the formulation of modern nation-states.

## 2. Historiographical Approaches
The interpretation of history is heavily influenced by the [[lens]] through which it is viewed. Over time, several distinct schools of historiography have emerged, particularly concerning Indian history:
*   **Colonial/Imperialist Historiography:** Propagated by British administrators and scholars like James Mill and V.A. Smith. This school often justified colonial rule by portraying pre-colonial India as stagnant, despotic, and lacking a sense of national unity until British intervention. 
*   **Nationalist Historiography:** Emerged as a reaction to colonial narratives. Historians like R.C. Majumdar and K.P. Jayaswal emphasized the glorious past of ancient India, highlighting achievements in science, statecraft, and philosophy to foster national pride during the freedom struggle.
*   **Marxist Historiography:** Pioneered by scholars like D.D. Kosambi, R.S. Sharma, and Romila Thapar. This approach shifted the focus from dynastic history to socio-economic formations, emphasizing class struggles, modes of production, and material conditions as the primary drivers of historical change.
*   **Subaltern Historiography:** Initiated by Ranajit Guha, this school focuses on the history from below—highlighting the roles, rebellions, and consciousness of the marginalized, such as peasants, tribals, and the working class, rather than focusing solely on elite leadership.

## 3. Classification of Historical Sources

Historians rely on diverse source materials to reconstruct the past. These are broadly categorized into Primary and Secondary sources:

| Source Type | Definition | Key Characteristics | Examples |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Primary Sources** | First-hand, contemporary accounts or physical evidence from the period under study. | Objective, unedited, and directly connected to the event or era. | Archaeological artifacts, inscriptions, coins, original manuscripts, government records, eyewitness diaries, photographs. |
| **Secondary Sources** | Later analyses, interpretations, or syntheses based on primary sources. | Subjective, analytical, written after the event by someone who was not present. | Textbooks, biographies, historical monographs, review articles, documentaries. |

### Trivia & Key Facts
*   **Herodotus**, a Greek historian from the 5th century BCE, is widely regarded as the "Father of History" for his [[work]] *The Histories*, which systematically documented the Greco-Persian Wars.
*   **Megasthenes**, the Greek ambassador to the court of [[Chandragupta]] Maurya, provided one of the earliest foreign accounts of India in his [[work]] *Indica* (a primary source, though the original is lost and it survives only in fragments quoted by later writers).
*   **Historiography** literally translates to "the writing of history" and involves the critical examination of sources, the selection of particulars from the authentic materials, and the synthesis of particulars into a narrative that will stand the test of critical methods.
